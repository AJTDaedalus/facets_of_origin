# Mirror Master's Manual: Session Design

Nobody remembers a well-structured session. They remember the bit where Mordai punched the archivist, and they remember it partly because you knew when to stop describing the archive.

Running a session is four jobs under one title: **shaping** it (giving three hours a beginning, a middle and a reason to come back), **pacing** it (knowing a scene is finished before the players do), **improvising** when the plan meets the players, and **spreading the light** so everyone at the table gets a turn at being the reason things worked. This chapter takes them one at a time, and on the way it covers the two procedures that carry most of a session between the fights: the exploration turn with its Pressure die, and the judgment calls magic asks of you.

> **MM Note — where to see this chapter working**
>
> Everything here is easier to read in a finished session than in the abstract. **Oraga Night** (`adventures/oraga_night/`) is this project's worked example: a night divided into Movements, scheduled events the party can't stop, open time they can spend how they like, and an omen in each Movement that rewards attention. Read its Overture beside *Session Structure* and *Pacing* in this chapter. The mapping is close to line for line.

## Session Structure

### The Three-Act Shape

Most good sessions have a shape, even when the players don't know they're inside one.

**Act I — the hook (10–20 minutes).** Open with something that demands a response. Not a briefing, not a recap: a situation. The tavern is on fire. The merchant they were meant to meet is already dead. The map they stole last session has a new line on it. If your opening lets the party shrug and go shopping, it isn't a hook, it's scenery. Three openings that almost always work: start in the middle of the action and skip the walk there; start with a consequence of last session, so the world has visibly moved; or start with a question — something is missing, or wrong, or different — and let curiosity do the rest.

**Act II — the middle (most of the session).** The party explores, investigates, fights, argues, makes plans and abandons them. Act II is a run of scenes, each one raising the stakes or revealing something new. Your job here is pacing and transitions: when a scene has given everything it has, move on; when the players are deep in something rich, let it breathe.

**Act III — the climax or the cliffhanger (15–30 minutes).** End with something that matters: a fight, a revelation, a choice with no clean answer, a door opening onto something unexpected. The best endings aren't settled so much as poised. The party has just learned or committed to or survived something, and the implications haven't finished landing.

Some sessions end in resolution. The quest is done, everyone's safe, and that's fine. Even then, give the last minute a beat that points forward: a letter arrives, a favor is called in, a detail from earlier turns out to mean something else.

### When to Break the Shape

The three acts are a default, not a cage. A session that's all middle (three hours of exploring a city, building friendships, chasing personal goals) can be one of the best you run. A session that's one long climax is memorable precisely because it broke the pattern. Use the shape when you need it and ignore it when the table is already where it needs to be.

## Pacing

Pacing is the most important thing you'll learn to do, and a book can only hand you the tools. Knowing when to use them comes from running games.

### When to Call for a Roll

Call for dice when three things are all true:

1. The outcome is genuinely uncertain.
2. Something changes whatever the result.
3. Success and failure would both be interesting.

If you're calling for a roll because a player tried something and you don't know what else to do, stop. Either it works (say so and move on) or it doesn't for a reason in the fiction (say why, and let them try something else).

**Don't roll for things that should just happen.** A character's **Specialty** already covers this for their narrow field: routine and informational tasks inside it simply happen, and the risky ones are Easy (see Chapter II.6). Outside a Specialty, apply the same sense. A strong character doesn't roll to climb a ladder, and nobody rolls to find the letter that's sitting on the desk.

The fastest test is to ask yourself, before the dice come out, what happens on a 6−. If you can't think of anything interesting, don't roll. If the answer is "nothing," definitely don't.

### Setting Difficulty

Every roll is Standard until something in the fiction says otherwise. Say the difficulty before the dice move, and when you shift it, say why.

**Table MM2–1: Setting Difficulty**

| Difficulty | Modifier | Sounds like | Stat +0 gets 7+ | Stat +2 gets 7+ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Easy | +1 | Clear advantage: time, tools, a sleepy guard | 72% | 92% |
| Standard | +0 | The default: uncertain, no edge either way | 58% | 83% |
| Hard | −1 | Able opposition, a storm, one hand free | 42% | 72% |
| Very Hard | −2 | Barely possible: the impossible leap, the king's own guard | 28% | 58% |

Three habits keep difficulty honest.

**Start at Standard, every time.** The most common mistake new MMs make is setting everything Hard because it feels dramatic. Hard is for when something in the scene is actively making it harder, and you should be able to say what in one clause: "Hard, because she's expecting you." If you can't name it, it's Standard.

**One step, rarely two.** Very Hard is for moments the table will talk about afterward. If you find yourself reaching for it twice a session, the fiction is telling you something about the plan, and the kinder move is to say so before anyone rolls.

**The character's side is already priced.** A knack adds +1 when it applies, never more than one knack at once, and a character's stat, knack and talent bonuses together never go past +4 (see Chapter III.1). Don't lower the difficulty as well because the character is good at this. Difficulty describes the situation, not the person.

### Something Always Happens on a 6−

On a 6−, something always happens. That's the whole point of it. A 6− isn't "you fail and nothing changes." It's "the situation moves in a direction nobody planned."

- The lock doesn't just stay locked: the pick snaps off inside it, and now nobody opens it the easy way.
- The guard doesn't just see you: she raises the alarm, and now the whole building knows.
- The haggling doesn't just fail: the merchant realizes how badly you need this, and the price doubles.

A 6− that stops the story is the MM's mistake, not the dice's. If failing a roll would leave the party with nowhere to go, the roll should never have been called. And a 6− is also the player's opening to claim a **Graceful Fail**: narrate the failure into something worse or richer, and you confirm the Spark (see Chapter III.1). Most of the time, say yes.

When your mind goes blank, and it will, the **Trouble** table in MM6 gives you six kinds of move in a single d6: cost, position, attention, gear, harm, revelation. Pick the row that fits, or roll it. Then say the consequence, never the category. "Attention" isn't something you say out loud; "the sergeant on the catwalk stops walking" is.

### A 7–9 Is a Cost, Not a Penalty

On a 7–9 the character **gets what they wanted**, and something else comes with it. Name the cost as part of narrating the success, one package, and pick it before you open your mouth. The complication tables in MM6 (fight, exploration, social and magic) each hold dozens of ready costs for exactly this.

The trap is the cost that costs nothing: "you get it, but they're a bit annoyed." Do that six times and the table learns that 7–9 is free, and a whole third of the dice stops meaning anything. The opposite trap is the cost that quietly takes the success away. The test for a good cost is simple: *does the table now have something to do?* A new problem, a new person, a new decision. If they're only slightly worse off and nothing else has changed, pick again.

Rotate the kinds of cost. If the last four were all favors owed, the favor has become a flat fee.

### Reading the Table's Energy

The players will tell you everything you need to know about pacing, but not in words. Watch what they do.

**The table is engaged** when players talk over each other in a good way, someone takes notes without being asked, plans refer back to details you mentioned, or somebody says "wait, what if we…" When that's happening, your job is to stay out of the way. Don't interrupt a good argument with narration, and don't call for a roll while they're building toward something.

**The table is drifting** when phones come out, answers get shorter, the same question gets asked twice, or the jokes are about the situation instead of inside it. When that's happening, change something. Not louder: different. Cut to another scene, have someone arrive, call for a roll with real stakes, or roll the Pressure die.

### Dragging or Building?

The hardest call in pacing is one question: **are the players stuck, or are they savoring?**

A scene is **dragging** when the players are repeating ideas, the information they need isn't here, the emotional beat has already landed, or you're narrating to fill time. Cut it: "The sun's setting by the time you leave the market. Where do you go?"

A scene is **building** when they're finding new details, the stakes are climbing through their own choices, someone's about to decide something that matters, or the quiet is the pause before someone speaks. Then say less. Answer their questions and react to their choices.

### Threat Clocks

Not every source of pressure is a person. Rising water, a spreading fire, a ritual counting down: Chapter III.2 handles these with a **Threat Clock**, a four-segment tracker the whole table can see. It advances one segment on every 7–9 or 6− rolled near the danger, and when it fills, the thing happens. A character can spend their action to wind it back one segment, with no roll.

What matters for you is the shape. A four-segment clock fills in about five or six rolls, which is roughly one scene. That makes it the cleanest way to put a timer on a scene with no fight in it: the search that has to finish before the tide comes in, the negotiation going on while the building burns. It does the work of urgency without you narrating urgency, because the table can watch it tick.

Two pieces of craft make clocks better. **Name the clock after the danger, not the solution.** "The tide," not "get the chest out," so the players can attack it any way they like. And **give each segment a line** of what the danger looks like as it grows: the water at the ankles, the knees, the waist, the ceiling. That turns a counter into a picture. If a clock is still half empty long after the scene should have ended, settle it in the fiction or let it fill. A clock that lingers stops being pressure and becomes furniture.

## Exploration Turns and the Pressure Die

Exploration is where many tables lose the thread: the party pokes around a ruin, nothing much happens, and the evening sags. The fix is a clock that isn't a timer, and a die that's never blank.

### The Exploration Turn

When the party is in a dangerous place, time passes in **exploration turns**. A turn is however long it takes to do one real thing: search a room, cross a cavern, pick a lock, argue about which passage to take. You don't count minutes. You notice that a thing got done, and that's a turn (see Chapter III.2 for the player's side of this).

At the end of each turn, roll the **Pressure die**, a single d6 whose every face means something:

1. **Encounter.** Something arrives or is found.
2. **Sign.** Evidence of what's coming: tracks, a noise, a smell, a fire still warm.
3. **Local hazard.** The place itself turns against them.
4. **Cost.** Something runs down: a usage die is rolled, time slips, a supply thins.
5. **Opportunity.** A lucky find, a shortcut, someone who needs help and could help back.
6. **Quiet.** Nothing troubles them. Give them one detail worth noticing.

The full table, with four variants for underground, wild, settlement and social occasions, is in MM6. Roll it at three moments: at the end of each exploration turn, at each leg of a journey, and when the party takes a breather somewhere that isn't safe.

### Why It Works

The Pressure die does four jobs with one roll. It's the wandering-monster check, the hazard timer, the supply drain and the lucky break, all at once. And none of its faces is empty. Even a 6 hands you a detail to describe.

It also teaches the players the place's rhythm without you saying a word. After the third roll they know that time spent here costs something, and they start planning faster. That's the pressure. You didn't have to raise your voice.

### Running It Well

**The sign comes first.** When you roll a 2, decide what the encounter is going to be, and show them its sign. The next time a 1 comes up, it's that thing. Tracks before the wolf, smoke before the bandits, the smell of wet fur before the thing in the well. A party that sees the sign and prepares has earned the edge that gives them.

**Write the site's own faces.** Faces 1 and 3 are the ones that make a place feel like itself. Before a session, write one line for each: *1 — the drowned sexton, lantern still lit; 3 — the flood gates groan and the water rises a foot.* It takes two minutes, and the ruin becomes this ruin rather than any ruin.

**Hold the costs early.** For the first two or three turns in a new place, treat a 4 as quiet. Nobody's torch should gutter in the first room. After that, let the costs land.

**A result is a prompt, not an order.** If the die says encounter and it would wreck a scene the players are enjoying, hold it for a turn. Don't hold it forever; the point of the die is that you don't get to choose.

**Usage dice.** Face 4 usually means rolling a **usage die**. Torches, rations, arrows and oil carry a die that runs from d8 to d6 to d4 and then to nothing. Roll it after a scene of use or when the Pressure die calls for it; on a 1 or 2 it steps down a size (see Chapter IV.1). It's the only supply tracking this game does, and it's plenty.

### Journeys

Travel works the same way, one size larger. Break a journey into **legs**, roughly a day or half a day each, and roll the Pressure die once per leg, using the wild or settlement variant as fits. An encounter on the road might be a fight, a meeting (roll reaction; see MM1) or a place worth visiting. A quiet leg takes one sentence: "Two days of good road and bad inns." Say it and move on. Some talents change the roll (*Pathfinder* guides with fewer rolls, *Scout's Eye* rolls twice and picks), so check the sheets before a long trip.

> **Example — three turns under the mill**
>
> The party is exploring a flooded undercroft. The MM has written two lines for this place: *1 — an eel the length of a boat; 3 — the sluice gate slips another inch.*
>
> **Turn one.** Zahna studies the carvings on a pillar. The MM rolls the underground Pressure die: 4, a cost. It's the first turn, so the MM treats it as quiet and describes the drip of water off the ceiling instead.
>
> **Turn two.** Mordai wades toward the far door. The die shows 2, a sign. The MM decides the sign belongs to the eel.
>
> MM: "Something heavy moves under the water about ten feet to your left. The ripples reach your knees a second later."
>
> **Mordai:** "I keep going."
>
> (Of course he does.)
>
> **Turn three.** Zulnut, very sensibly, climbs onto a ledge to look around. The die shows 3, the local hazard. The sluice gate slips, and the water rises to Mordai's waist. The eel now has considerably more room to work with, and the party has a decision to make about how badly they want to reach that door.

## Scenes

A session is made of scenes. Knowing what kind of scene you're in, and what kind the table needs next, is how you keep a session moving without it feeling rushed.

### Exploration

**Describe three things.** When the party enters a new space, give them three concrete details: something they see, hear, smell or feel. Three is enough to be vivid. More than three and you're writing prose; fewer and the space is empty.

**Then let them ask.** Their questions show you what matters to them. Build the scene around those questions, not around your notes.

**Searching is a conversation, not a roll.** "I check behind the painting" is an action, and if something's behind the painting, they find it. Roll when the place is actively dangerous, when time pressure makes the search uncertain, or when expertise really does decide what's noticed.

### Social

**Play the person, not the numbers.** An NPC is someone with wants, fears and a reason to be here. When the party talks to them, you're playing a person reacting to what these strangers say. The dice confirm or complicate the fiction; they don't replace it.

**Roll reaction when you don't know how they feel.** A stranger on the road, a guard at the gate, the thing in the cellar: roll 2d6 and read the band (see MM1). Most results are *uncertain*, and uncertain means they want something first.

**Roll only at the pivot.** If a conversation is going well, don't interrupt it with dice. Call for a roll at the moment of commitment: the player asks for something the NPC wouldn't freely give, the lie reaches breaking point, the argument has to land.

**Know where they won't go.** Every NPC has something no roll will get from them. The loyal guard won't betray her captain; the grieving parent won't forgive. Decide that before the scene, so "she won't do that" never sounds arbitrary.

**A social 7–9 costs something real.** They know you needed it; you owe a favor; someone overheard; you got the part that cost them least. The Social Complications table in MM6 has thirty-six of these. And a social 6− is almost never a plain no, because a no ends the scene. Make it a worse relationship than the one they walked in with: they agree and mean something different, or they refuse and tell someone why you asked.

### Investigation

Investigation lives on one principle: **the players must be able to find the clues they need to move forward.** That doesn't make every clue free. It means the information is always reachable by some route. If the only way forward sits behind one roll, and the roll fails, the session has a structural problem.

**The three-clue rule.** For any conclusion you need the players to reach, place at least three clues that point to it, each reachable a different way. One will be missed. One will be misread. The third confirms or corrects. If the villain is hiding in the old mill: a witness saw someone heading for the river at night, the stolen goods include milling gear, and the ransom note has flour on it. Social, investigative, physical. They'll find at least one, probably two, and when they kick in the mill door they'll have earned it.

**Show, don't gate.** "You find the letter" is narration. "Roll to recognize the seal" is a roll that adds depth. The letter exists either way.

### Combat

MM1 covers building and running fights. The pacing note for this chapter: **combat should take less time at the table than the players expect.** A fight of three exchanges should take fifteen to twenty-five minutes. If it's running longer, the fight has too many moving parts, the players need help seeing their options, or it should have ended an exchange ago. When the question's been answered, narrate the end.

### Downtime

Downtime is where the characters get to be people: resting, mending, pursuing their own goals. Go around the table and ask each player what their character is doing right now. Not "what do you want to accomplish," which sounds like a task list. Quiet players often shine here, when they aren't competing for airtime, and downtime is where a lot of the best Spark nominations come from.

### Moving Between Scenes

The smoothest transitions are the ones nobody notices. The party finds someone, so describe them before they speak. The talk breaks down, so telegraph what the foes are about to do. The fight ends: what's left in the room? A clue points to a person: where are they? Any scene into downtime takes two words: "Night falls."

**Skip the walk.** When the current scene is only setting up the next one, cut to the arrival. "You reach the tower. It's taller than the reports said."

**Let the good ones breathe.** When the players are doing something you didn't plan and it's working, when the roleplay is richer than whatever you had next, don't cut it for the sake of pacing. Those moments are why anyone plays.

## Improvising

You'll improvise more than you prepare. Accept that now and it stops being frightening.

### Yes, And; Yes, But; No, But

**"Yes, and…"** The idea works and makes something new. *"Is there a chandelier?" "Yes, and it's hanging by a single chain that's about fifteen years past its last inspection."*

**"Yes, but…"** The idea works, at a price. This is the 7–9 of conversation. *"Can I bribe the guard?" "Yes, but he takes the coin and tells you forgetting your face will cost twice that."*

**"No, but…"** The idea doesn't work as stated, and something else is on offer. *"I kick the door down." "It's iron, it's not going anywhere. But the wall beside it is crumbling plaster, and you can hear voices through it."*

Plain **"no"** is for when the fiction really doesn't allow it. When you say it, say why, and point at what's possible instead.

### Prep Situations, Not Plots

A **plot** is a sequence: the party meets the informant, learns about the warehouse, raids the warehouse, finds the evidence. Skip the informant and it breaks.

A **situation** is a set of pressures: smugglers are moving something dangerous through a warehouse, the watch is bought, and the informant who knows the details is scared. The party can come at it from any side, and the situation responds. To prep one, answer three questions. **Who wants what?** **What happens if the party does nothing?** (That's your clock.) **Where can they push?** Have several answers to the last one, and no favorite.

### Prep by the Clock

You don't have four hours. Almost nobody does. So here's prep by what you've actually got, and each step includes the one before.

**Twenty minutes.** Decide the one thing that's changed since last session, and who it changed for. Pick your opening scene. Roll five names from the NPC Names table in MM6 and write them down. That's enough to run three hours.

**One hour.** Add the situation on a page: who wants what, what happens if the party does nothing, three places they could push. Write one monster card (MM1) for the push they're most likely to make, and the local faces 1 and 3 of the Pressure die for the main location.

**Two hours.** Add three clues to whatever you want them to work out, a list of a few secrets they might learn in any scene, a set piece with its terrain written as things the party can use, and a second card for the push you think is unlikely. You'll be wrong about which one is unlikely about half the time.

**Four hours or more.** Stop. Past this point prep starts producing material you're attached to, and material you're attached to is material you'll steer the party toward.

**Under twenty minutes.** Pick an opening scene and ask the table what their characters have been thinking about since last time. Build the first hour from their answers. This is a real method, not an emergency measure. And when there's no time at all, cancel. A session you resent prepping is one the table can feel.

### When You're Stuck

It happens to everyone: the party has solved what you prepared, or wandered off, and it's only nine o'clock. The app's **Stuck?** button, and the procedure in MM6 behind it, gives you three options, drawn from your own prep wherever it can:

- **A threat moves.** Tick a Threat Clock, or show the next step of something the villain is doing.
- **Someone arrives with a want.** An NPC from your cast, or a fresh one from the NPC tables, walks in needing something from the party.
- **A secret surfaces.** One of the things they could learn comes to them: a letter, a slip of the tongue, a dream.

Pick one. Don't pick the one that's most dramatic; pick the one that fits the room. Any of them will get the table moving.

## Adjudicating Magic

Magic in this game has no spell list, so there's no table to hide behind. When a player says "I want to pull the heat out of the air in front of the door," you answer three questions: is it inside their domain, how big is it, and, when the dice land in the middle, what does it cost. The player does most of the work. They name the domain, say the intent and declare the scope. You confirm, set the number, and roll with them.

The habit worth building above all others: **rule out loud, before the dice.** Say the scope, say the difficulty, then roll. A ruling given after the result always sounds like an adjustment.

### Judging Scope

Scope is set by two things and only two: **how much of the world changes, and for how long.** It isn't set by how impressive the effect looks, how many words the player used, or how clever it was.

- **Minor** — small, local, brief. One object or creature, the immediate area, a moment. It never deals damage and never decides anything; in a fight, it's a stunt.
- **Significant** — meaningful power or precision. A door fused for the scene, a person bound, one foe struck.
- **Major** — scene-changing. A building, a crowd, a group of foes, an effect that endures.

The rule that settles most arguments: **meaningful power or precision is never Minor.** If the working would decide a scene or hurt someone, it's at least Significant, however modestly the player describes it. Chapter II.3 gives each scope its difficulty and its Fatigue cost; Major needs level 3.

> **Example — one glyph, two scopes**
>
> **Zahna:** "I inscribe a hold-glyph across the door seam so it won't open while we get clear."
>
> MM: "A door held shut for the scene. That's Significant: Standard, one Fatigue. Roll Mind."
>
> **Zahna:** "Actually, I want it to hold after we're gone. Days, if it can."
>
> MM: "Then it's a ward, and it lasts. Major, which is Hard, and two Fatigue. Same door, same glyph. You're just asking it to outlive you."
>
> (Nothing about the glyph changed. Duration alone moved it up a scope, and that's correct: duration is one of the two measures.)

**When you raise a scope, say the new price and wait.** The player may want to scale the intent back down: a narrower area, a shorter time, one target instead of a room. That trade of ambition against cost is theirs to make. Say "that's Major, Hard, two Fatigue," then give them two seconds.

### Domain Boundaries

A domain is territory agreed at character creation and used consistently after that. When a request is genuinely borderline, **the default is yes.** Not "yes if they argue well." Yes.

What separates a borderline yes from a real no isn't how far the request reaches, it's *what it reaches through.* Does it run through the substance of the domain, or does it only rhyme with the domain's mood? Fire burning the air out of a room is substance: fire and air really are related, and the caster is working on that relationship. Fire commanding the weather is rhyme: fire is hot and storms are loud, and that's the whole connection.

**Don't charge extra for the stretch.** A borderline yes is priced like any other working, by scope. **A yes is a precedent**, since the boundary you draw tonight is the boundary next month, which is a reason to be generous rather than cautious. **A no is a "no, but":** "Your fire won't reach the storm. There's a lot of dry timber between you and that gate, though, and the wind is already doing half your work."

If the same boundary gets renegotiated every session, stop ruling on it mid-game. Have the conversation once, between sessions, and write down what you agree.

### The 7–9 and the 6−

On a 7–9, **the working works.** Then the app offers the caster two costs from the Magic Complications table in MM6 and the player picks one. If you're at a paper table, roll twice and let them choose. Narrate the cost as part of the success: *"The glyph bit exactly where you wanted it. It also went off like a cannon, and whatever lives on the floor above now knows exactly where you are."*

On a 6−, the Fatigue is spent anyway, and something from Magic Mishaps happens. That table is written to make things worse and more interesting, never to kill the scene. It's also a natural spot for a Graceful Fail: the player who leans into the mishap has earned a Spark.

### Fatigue and Rest

A Significant working costs 1 **Fatigue** and a Major one costs 2. Each point fills an inventory slot until a night's rest clears it (see Chapter II.3). A caster with no free slot can't cast a full working. Minor workings cost nothing. Heavy armor adds 1 Fatigue to every full working, and signature workings are one step Easier.

That makes rest the one lever the magic system gives you directly. **A night's rest in safety** restores all HP, clears all Fatigue and clears one Wound. Whether a night is safe is your call, and it's a real one. A locked inn room is safe. A camp in hostile country is a breather at best, and the Pressure die gets a vote. Say the call out loud, before the casters decide how much to spend, so the table knows what the next stretch will cost.

## Spreading the Light

Every player deserves moments where the game is about their character. Not near their character: about them.

### The Spotlight Check

When you're not sure the light is spread fairly, check two things. **Who spoke last?** If the same player has started the last three actions, someone else needs a moment. **Who earned the last Spark?** If one player earns them every session and another hasn't in three, the second isn't getting moments that show off what their character does.

Neither means you're doing it wrong. They're signals. Use them.

### Quiet and Loud

**Quiet players** aren't necessarily disengaged. Some people play by watching and speak when they have something specific to say. Don't force them to perform. Ask their character something specific instead of "what do you do?": "Zahna, you've been reading the inscription on the wall. Does anything in it bother you?" Put problems in front of the party that only their character can solve. And use downtime, where they aren't competing for the floor.

**Loud players** usually aren't stealing the light; they're filling the silence other people leave. Redirect rather than suppress: "Mordai, you're mid-swing, but Zulnut, the guard's running for the bell. What do you do?" Split the party briefly, so everyone gets a scene. Or make the loud player's action the setup: Mordai's shouting draws every eye in the room, and that's when Zulnut's hands get busy.

### Sparks as a Balancing Tool

Encourage the table to nominate each other for Sparks. When one player calls "Spark?" for another, two things happen: the nominated player feels seen, and the table celebrates what they did. If nominations keep landing on the same player, that's information. Maybe they're playing brilliantly. Maybe nobody else is getting moments worth nominating.

## Spark Cadence

Every character starts every session with 3 Sparks, and unspent Sparks don't carry over. The failure mode is hoarding: players treat three Sparks as too precious to spend, and the dice go unhelped all night. The cure is earning often enough that spending feels safe.

The four ways to earn one (MM award, peer call, act-break nomination and the Graceful Fail) are in Chapter III.1. Your job is to make them happen at a steady beat:

- **Call for nominations at every act break.** Each player may nominate one other player for something in the scene just past. Confirm almost every one; this is celebration, not gatekeeping.
- **Confirm Graceful Fails promptly.** The player claims it off their own 6−. Say yes unless they're gaming it.
- **Award Sparks for weakness played at a cost.** Mordai charging in when the situation plainly calls for caution, because that's who Mordai is. That rewards character, not optimization.
- **If someone hasn't spent a Spark by the session's midpoint,** put a moment in front of them where spending one would matter.

**Table MM2–2: Spark Target Economy**

| Session | Each player starts | Earns | Spends |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quiet | 3 | 1–2 | 1–2 |
| Typical | 3 | 2–3 | 3–4 |
| Heavy fighting | 3 | 3–4 | 4–6 |

The target is spend what you earn. Sparks should feel like a renewing resource, not a savings account.

## Session Zero

Session zero is the session before the first one: the conversation where everyone agrees what game they're playing and how. It prevents most problems before they start. Cover four things.

**Safety.** Lines (content that won't appear at all) and veils (content that happens off-screen), plus whatever tool lets anyone pause the game without explaining why. MM4 covers these in full.

**Tone.** The default here is adventure: wonder, heroism, discovery. Say whether this campaign sits there or somewhere darker, and agree it together. Say plainly how dangerous the world is, too. In these rules, a character at 0 HP takes a Wound and rolls to hold on; death only ever comes through a choice the player makes (see Chapter III.2). Tell the table that before it matters.

**The pitch.** One paragraph: the starting situation, the kind of trouble the party will face, the scope, roughly how long it will run. Enough for players to build characters who belong in the story (see MM3).

**Connections.** At least one link between each pair of characters, one thing the whole party went through together before the campaign starts, and one thing each character wants that the campaign could give them.

## The App in Session

The app exists to carry the bookkeeping so you don't have to. During a session you'll mostly live in the **Play** tab: the character sheets with their HP, slots, Wounds and Fatigue; the roll, attack and cast controls; the Sparks; and your combat tracker, where foes' attacks roll in the open with a click and morale and Bloodied are flagged for you.

The **Toolbox** panel is the MM's side of the screen. Reaction, the Pressure die with its variants, the complication tables, loot, NPCs, the oracle, and the **Stuck?** button are all there, all one or two clicks from the scene, and all private until you choose to show a result. Every result is a suggestion. Reroll it, hold it for later or ignore it; the app never applies one for you.

At the end of each session the app shows you the five level-up questions (see MM3). Answer them with the table, and call the level when it's time.

What the app should never do is stop the game. Don't pause a session to rebuild a character or dig through a menu; make a note and do it at the break. The Build tab is for session zero and level-ups, not for the middle of a fight.

## In Play: The Price of a Partial Success

*The party has tracked a smuggling operation to a warehouse on the docks. Zahna found the building in the records; Mordai confirmed it with a watch sergeant who owed him a favor. It's past midnight.*

MM: "Two floors, stone below and timber above. A loading door facing the water, shut. A side door off the alley. One shuttered window upstairs. No light, but you can hear something heavy being moved inside."

**Zulnut:** "How many voices?"

MM: "Roll Mind. Standard."

→ Zulnut rolls **2d6 + Mind (+0)** and gets a **9**. Success, with a cost.

MM: "At least three. One is giving orders, and the other two aren't arguing, which is either discipline or fear. And you've been crouched by that wall long enough that someone across the street has noticed you."

(The cost: attention. The MM names it with the success, in the same breath.)

**Mordai:** "Side door. I'll go first."

**Zahna:** "We don't know what's inside."

**Mordai:** "Smugglers."

**Zahna:** "We don't know what they're smuggling."

**Mordai:** "Does it change the door?"

(Zahna opens his mouth, then closes it. The man has a point.)

**Zulnut:** "I'll do the lock."

MM: "A simple padlock, but you're picking it in the dark while people move around on the other side. Standard. Roll Body."

→ Zulnut rolls **2d6 + Body (+2)** and gets a **7**. Success, with a cost.

MM: "It opens. The hasp squeaks, one sharp note that carries exactly as far as you didn't want it to. The movement inside stops."

*Three seconds of silence. Then the sound of something being set down very carefully.*

**Mordai** pushes the door open. "Good evening."

MM: "Crates along the walls, a clear space in the middle, and three men halfway through moving something from a big crate into small ones. Two reach for short blades. The third doesn't reach for anything. He's looking at you like someone working out whether a fight is cheaper than a conversation."

(The MM rolls reaction for the one who's thinking: a 7, uncertain. He'll talk, but he wants something first.)

**Zahna:** "What are they moving?"

MM: "There's a faint glow from the open crate. Not light. The color of something that shouldn't glow."

**Zahna:** "That's a restricted reagent. They're not smuggling goods, they're smuggling workings."

(The MM had planned that reveal for session three. Zahna got there in one and a half.)

MM: "The thinking man says, 'Gentlemen. This isn't what it looks like. Perhaps we could talk outside, where the merchandise is less… atmospheric?'"

**Mordai:** "I want to know who he works for."

MM: "Soul, and it's Hard. He's calm, it's his ground, and he's had thirty seconds to get ready for this conversation. Does *City Watch* apply?"

**Mordai:** "I've questioned smugglers before."

MM: "Fair. Plus one."

→ Mordai rolls **2d6 + Soul (+1) + knack (+1)**, Hard (−1), and gets an **8**. Success, with a cost.

MM: "He tells you it's a private arrangement, and the buyer isn't someone whose name gets said in warehouses. He's telling the truth; you can feel it. And now he's looking at you with real concern. Not for himself. For you. 'Walk away,' he says. 'This is bigger than a warehouse. I'm doing you a favor.'"

(Cost: the truth, and it's worse than the lie Mordai expected.)

**Mordai:** "That's never worked on me."

*The two men with blades look at their boss. He hasn't told them to fight. He hasn't told them to stand down. The crate glows quietly between them.*

(The MM ends the session there. The players spend four days in the group chat arguing about whether the smuggler was telling the truth. He was.)

## Quick Reference: Session Design

```
BEFORE THE SESSION
- Review last session: what happened, what was promised, what was left open
- Prep a situation, not a plot: who wants what, what if nobody acts, where to push
- Roll five names; write the site's Pressure die faces 1 and 3
- Is anyone due a spotlight moment?

DURING THE SESSION
- Open on a hook that demands a response
- Roll only when uncertain, consequential, and interesting both ways
- Start every difficulty at Standard; say why when you move it
- 7-9: they get it, plus a cost that gives the table something to do
- 6-: something always happens; offer the Graceful Fail
- Exploration turn done? Roll the Pressure die
- Call for Spark nominations at every act break
- Cut scenes that drag; let the good ones breathe
- End on a moment that points forward

AFTER THE SESSION
- The five level-up questions (MM3)
- Note facts, promises and what the players cared about
- Refill the names
```
