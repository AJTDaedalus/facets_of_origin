# Rules: Adventuring

This chapter covers what happens between fights: moving through dangerous places, the hazards that close in while you do, how you recover, and what happens when a character's life is on the line. It all runs on the roll from Chapter III.1. The only new die is the MM's.

---

## Exploration

Outside a fight, the game moves at the speed of the story. When the party is somewhere dangerous (a ruin, a cave, a haunted wood) the MM slows time into **exploration turns**. A turn is as long as it takes to do one real thing: search a room, cross a flooded hall, pick a lock with care, argue about which corridor.

### The Pressure Die

At the end of each exploration turn, each leg of a journey, and each breather taken somewhere dangerous, the MM rolls the **Pressure die**: a single d6, read against a short table (MM6). It can bring an encounter, a sign of something nearby, a hazard, a cost (a torch gutters, the rations get wet), an opportunity, or a quiet stretch. Places have their own versions: a cave's Pressure die reads differently from a city's.

For players, the Pressure die means one thing: **time is never free somewhere dangerous.** Searching every room twice is safe in a library. In a ruin it has a price, and the price is rolled in the open.

### Journeys and Supplies

A journey is a series of legs, each ending somewhere the party can stop. The MM rolls the Pressure die once per leg. Light, food and ammunition run on the usage die (Chapter IV.1): after a scene or a leg of using them, roll it, and on a 1 or 2 it shrinks.

### Meeting People

When the party meets someone whose attitude the fiction hasn't already fixed, the MM makes a **reaction roll**: 2d6 for their attitude, from hostile to friendly (MM6). Most people you meet are not looking for a fight. A good first sentence from you still counts for more than their roll.

## Hazards and Threat Clocks

Not every danger is an enemy with an attack. Rising water, a spreading fire, a ritual counting down: these are hazards, and each gets a **Threat Clock**, a four-segment tracker the whole table can see.

**It advances** one segment whenever a character rolls a **7–9 or a 6−** on a roll made near the hazard. A 10+ never advances it; that character handled things too cleanly for the hazard to gain ground. A group roll advances it at most once.

**When it fills**, the hazard strikes. The MM deals damage to everyone caught in it, hands out a Wound, or calls for an avoid roll (Chapter III.1), and says which before anyone rolls.

**Winding it back** costs a character their action, and nothing else. There is no roll. Shoring up the wall, bailing the water, holding the door: one segment back, automatically.

The wind-back never rolls because a roll could advance the very clock you were winding back, which is a trap nobody enjoys. Spending the action is the cost, and it always works.

> **MM Note — nobody plays the janitor**
>
> Because the wind-back always works, it can quietly become one character's job while everyone else has the adventure. Rotate it: let the fiction ask someone new to hold the door this time. And narrate a wind-back as vividly as any roll. The character who buys the party one more segment has earned the same spotlight as the one who rolled a 12.

A four-segment clock fills in five or six rolls near the hazard, because most rolls land on 7–9 or worse. That is the intended feel: a hazard should close in within a single scene.

## In Play: The Mill at Millhaven

*The investigation has finally led out of Thornwall and up the valley to Millhaven itself: to the mill the village is named for, which has not ground grain in fifteen years, and whose undercroft is where the trail ends.*

**MM:** "The undercroft is low, stone, and older than the mill above it. Floor grates along the west wall. A sluice gate in the corner, held shut by a winch and chain. A rack of rusted tools by the stairs. And the door you came through, which has swollen into its frame behind you in a way you are choosing not to think about. As Mordai steps off the stairs, the winch, which has held back the millpond since before any of you were born, snaps."

(A pause, for effect.)

**MM:** "Water starts coming up through the grates. This is a hazard, not a fight. Four-segment Threat Clock. When it fills, the water wins."

**Zahna:** "A millrace this old will have a relief valve. I look for it."

**MM:** "Mind. Standard."

→ Zahna rolls **2d6 + Mind (+2)** at Standard and gets an **8**. Partial success.

**MM:** "There's a valve housing on the east wall, behind a collapsed shelf of quern-stones. But finding it took time you didn't have. The water is over your boots." *One segment fills.*

**Mordai:** "I move the stones."

→ Mordai rolls **2d6 + Body (+2)** at Standard and gets a **7**. Partial success.

**MM:** "You move the stones. You also move the shelf, which turns out to have been holding up more than stones. The valve is clear, and the water is at your knees." *Two of four.*

**Zulnut:** "I'm not rolling anything. I take a pry bar off that tool rack and spend my whole action wedging the biggest grate shut."

**MM:** "That's winding it back. It costs your action and nothing else." *The clock ticks back to one.*

**Zulnut:** "The best kind of action. The kind that can't fail."

*It can't fail. It also can't last. The valve wheel is seized, a failure, and the second segment fills. The pry bar shears, a partial: three. Mordai's shoulder, applied directly to the problem, opens the valve and tears half the housing off the wall in one motion, a partial: four. The clock fills.*

**MM:** "The pond finds the gap where the housing was, and the surge takes all three of you off your feet. That's the hazard striking: 4 damage each, and armor doesn't help against a millpond." *Mordai drops to 12, Zulnut to 8. Zahna, who started on 6, is at 2 and would like it on record that he found the valve.* "The good news is the water now has somewhere to go that isn't this room. The bad news is the sound from directly overhead. The main beam under the mill floor has been soaking all scene, and it has just cracked like a mast."

## Rest and Recovery

**Table III.2–1: Recovery**

| Rest | What it takes | What it restores |
|---|---|---|
| Breather | A few quiet minutes. Somewhere dangerous, the MM rolls the Pressure die. | Half your maximum HP |
| Night's rest | A night in safety | All your HP, all your Fatigue, and one Wound |

A breather can be taken whenever the fiction allows a few quiet minutes. How many breathers a day the party gets is really a question of how many quiet minutes the world gives them, and the Pressure die is how the world answers.

---

## Hit Points and Wounds

**Hit points** are grit: stamina, nerve, luck, and the near-misses that wear you down. Losing HP means you are tiring and taking knocks, not that you are bleeding out. Damage in a fight is in Chapter III.3. Out of a fight, a fall, a fire or a hazard deals damage the MM sets, and armor helps only where armor would.

### At 0 HP

When damage takes you to **0 HP**, two things happen.

**You take a Wound.** The MM rolls it on the Wounds table (MM6): a cracked rib, a twisted knee, a cut over the eye. A Wound **fills one slot** in your inventory, and **rolls that strain it are Hard**. A night's rest clears one Wound; some talents clear more.

**You roll Hold On**: 2d6 + Body.

**Table III.2–2: Hold On**

| Total | Result |
|---|---|
| 10+ | You stand at 1 HP. |
| 7–9 | You are out of the fight, but conscious. |
| 6− | You are dying. An ally who tends you before the scene ends saves you. |

Being saved means you live, at 0 HP, with your Wound. A breather or a night's rest brings you back up from there.

### The Death Choice

If you are dying and nobody reaches you before the scene ends, the choice is **yours**, and nobody else's:

**Take a Scar.** You live, and you carry something permanent: roll on the Scars table (MM6), or, when the fiction has already made it obvious what the scar is, name it together with the MM instead. A Scar never heals. It is a fact of the world from then on.

**Choose a heroic death.** Your character takes one final action, and it **succeeds**. No roll, no difficulty, no failure. Whatever you attempt with that last action happens. Then your character dies.

A character in this game dies only when their player chooses the ending. Bad luck can put you on the floor. It can't write your last line.

> **Through the Mirror — why death is a choice**
>
> We want fights to be dangerous and deaths to be rare, and those pull against each other. Hit points and damage dice make a fight feel real. Hold On and an ally's hand make most falls temporary. The death choice handles the rest: when the dice have done their worst, the player decides whether this is a story about surviving or a story about an ending.
>
> Both choices cost something real. That is why neither feels like a consolation prize.

## In Play: The Beam

*The valve is open, the pond is draining through the undercroft instead of into it, and the party is scrambling for the stairs. The main beam goes as Zahna reaches the door. Mordai does what Mordai does: he steps under it.*

**MM:** "You catch it. Both hands, full squat, the wet weight of a mill floor across your shoulders, and it buys Zahna and Zulnut the doorway. You're still standing in the hazard, so I'm restarting the clock." *It advances on Zahna's scramble up the millrace, a partial: one. On Zulnut's, a partial: two. The stairs buckle half a step beneath them, a failure: three. The beam settles deeper: four.* "It fills. The floor comes down on the one person still under it. That's 12 damage, Mordai, and plate doesn't help against a building."

*Mordai was on 12. He is on 0.*

**MM:** "A Wound first." *The MM rolls it.* "Cracked ribs. Then Hold On: Body."

→ Mordai rolls **2d6 + Body (+2)** at Standard and gets a **6**. Failure.

**MM:** "You're dying. And I want to be precise here, because this is the one moment where precision matters most. An ally who reaches you before this scene ends saves you. But the floor is coming down and the water is at your chest, and nobody can get to you before it does. So the rules stop being mine and become yours. You can take a Scar, and Mordai lives. Or you can choose a heroic death: one final action, anything you can describe, and it succeeds. Then Mordai's story ends."

(The table has gone very quiet. Zulnut's player has put down the snacks.)

**Mordai:** "Are they clear?"

**MM:** "Zahna's through. Zulnut is on the threshold, looking back at you."

**Mordai:** "Then nobody needs the heroic version. I drop the beam, I dive for the millrace, and I take the Scar. The shoulder. The one that held it. Mordai's carried every heavy thing this party ever found. Now there's something he can't."

**MM:** "No need to roll the table for that one; the fiction has already named it. The shoulder sets wrong and stays that way. Cold mornings, it announces the weather. Write it down. It's permanent, and it's true."

*Mordai comes out of the millrace downstream, alive, coughing, holding his side. The mill floor comes down behind him. From now on it is simply a fact of the world: the strongest back in the party has one thing it will never carry again.*

**Zulnut:** "For the record, I'd have taken the heroic death. Extremely dramatic. No cleanup."

**MM:** (Nobody doubts it, Zulnut.)
