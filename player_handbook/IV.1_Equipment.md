# Compendium: Equipment

A greatsword and a dagger take the same bite out of an enemy's Resolve. What differs is everything else: what a room does when you walk into it carrying one, what you can hide under a coat, and what the fiction lets you attempt without anybody calling for a roll.

This chapter is short on purpose. Weapons pick which attribute you Strike with, armor is a budget you spend across a scene, and the rest of your gear matters exactly as much as the story you tell with it.

Equipment in Facets of Origin is narrative, not numerical. A weapon is not a stat block — it is a tool that shapes how your character engages with the world and what kind of stories they create. The system cares about what your equipment *means* for the fiction, not how many decimal points it contributes to your roll.

---

## Weapons

Weapons determine which attribute you use for a Strike. Choose the weapon that fits your character's fiction.

**Table IV.1–1: Weapon Categories**

| Category | Attribute | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Heavy** | Strength | Greatsword, warhammer, greataxe, maul |
| **Standard** | Strength or Dexterity (player's choice) | Longsword, mace, spear, staff, hand axe |
| **Light** | Dexterity | Dagger, short sword, rapier, hand crossbow |
| **Ranged** | Dexterity | Longbow, crossbow, sling, thrown weapons |
| **Unarmed** | Strength or Dexterity (player's choice) | Fists, grappling, martial arts, headbutts |

All weapons deal the same mechanical effect: a successful Strike depletes an enemy's Resolve — 2 on a full success, 1 on a partial — or, against another character, applies a Condition of the appropriate tier (Chapter III.3). There are no damage dice, no weapon damage tables, no +1 swords in the base rules. The fiction determines what the hit looks like; the outcome tiers determine the mechanical consequence.

> **Through the Mirror — why there are no damage numbers**
>
> Because tracking variable damage per weapon creates complexity without meaningful choice. A dagger and a greatsword take the same bite out of an enemy's Resolve on a 10+ — the fiction of *how* they do it is different, and that difference matters narratively, not arithmetically.

### Weapon Specialization

Characters with relevant Techniques (such as Weapon Mastery from the Body Facet tree) may gain additional benefits with specific weapon types. These are handled through the Technique system, not through weapon stat blocks.

## Armor

Armor reduces the severity of incoming Conditions. It does not add to your rolls or create a separate defense value.

> **Through the Mirror — why armor is a budget and not a subtraction**
>
> Damage reduction invites arithmetic on every blow, and arithmetic on every blow is the thing this game does not do. A per-scene budget asks one question instead — *is this the hit worth spending a charge on?* — and asks it a handful of times a fight rather than continuously.
>
> Enemy armor is a different tool wearing the same name: on a stat block it is flat extra Resolve. That is not an exception to this rule, it is a second rule for a second job, and the two never need reconciling at the table. Replace the budget with flat reduction and light armor stops being a decision and becomes a number nobody thinks about again.

On your character sheet, armor is a **downgrade budget**: a finite number of incoming Conditions it can soften per scene, each by one tier. Full rules — including how the budget interacts with reactions — are in Chapter III.3.

**Table IV.1–2: Armor**

| Armor | Effect | Fictional Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **None** | Conditions apply at full tier | Unencumbered, silent, mobile |
| **Light** | First **2** incoming Conditions per scene are downgraded one tier (Tier 2 becomes Tier 1; Tier 1 is negated) | Leather, padded, chain shirt — some noise, some weight |
| **Heavy** | First **4** incoming Conditions per scene are downgraded one tier | Plate, full chain, heavy brigandine — loud, hot, imposing |

The budget is spent across the whole scene and refills when the scene ends — two fights in one scene draw on the same budget. When it runs out, Conditions land at full tier. Protection is real, but a long enough fight spends through it.

### Armor in the Fiction

Armor has consequences beyond combat:

- **Light armor** is unobtrusive. It can be concealed under clothing. It does not meaningfully affect Stealth, social situations, or movement.
- **Heavy armor** announces your presence. It imposes noise, heat, and conspicuousness. The MM may increase difficulty on Stealth, Finesse, or social checks where heavy armor is a liability. It is not subtle, and it is not comfortable. It is, however, extremely good at keeping you in the fight.

## Armor in Play

> **Example — spending a downgrade budget**
>
> **MM:** The guardian's arm comes around at the height of your head. That's a Tier 2 Condition incoming — Named-and-worse hit hard.
>
> **Mordai:** Absorb. I'm not dodging that in plate.
>
> **MM:** Absorb costs nothing and you take it in full. Except you're in heavy armor, and that's four downgrades for the scene. Tier 2 becomes Tier 1 — you're Off-Balance, not Staggered. Three charges left.
>
> **Zulnut:** *(who is wearing a coat)* And if that had been me?
>
> **MM:** Staggered, and it persists until somebody treats it. That's the trade. You are much harder to hit and much worse at being hit.

The charge is spent when armor does the work. On a partial reaction — a 7–9 Dodge or Parry that already downgraded the blow — the armor was not what saved you, so the budget is untouched.

## Adventuring Gear

Adventuring gear is handled narratively rather than as an itemized inventory. If your character would reasonably have a piece of common equipment given their background and preparation, they have it. If it is unusual, specialized, or the MM decides supply matters in this moment, it becomes a fiction question.

### Common Gear (assumed available)

If your character is an adventurer who has had time to prepare, they carry:

- A weapon appropriate to their background
- A light source (torch, lantern, candle)
- Rope, fifty feet of it
- Basic rations and a waterskin
- A pack or satchel
- Tools of their trade (lockpicks for a thief, a journal for a scholar, a holy symbol for a priest)

### Specialized Gear

Items that are unusual, expensive, or narratively significant are tracked individually. These include:

- **Healing supplies** — bandages, poultices, or alchemical aids that allow treating Tier 2 Conditions outside of combat
- **Specialty tools** — a disguise kit, an alchemist's kit, cartographer's tools. Having the right tools may reduce the difficulty of relevant skill checks by one step
- **Magical items** — covered in a future chapter; these have specific, named effects

### The "Do You Have It?" Rule

When a player needs a piece of common gear not on their character sheet:

1. Is it reasonable that this character would carry it? (A former soldier probably has a whetstone. A scholar probably has ink.)
2. Did the character have time to prepare before the current situation?

If both answers are yes, they have it. If either is uncertain, the MM may call for a Luck roll — 10+ means you thought ahead, 7–9 means you have something close but not quite right, 6- means you definitely do not have it and the absence matters.

## Currency and Trade

The base rules do not include a detailed economy system. Currency exists in the fiction — characters can buy, sell, and trade — but prices, exchange rates, and wealth tracking are left to the MM's judgment and the needs of the story.

> **MM Note — Money and wealth**
>
> If money matters in your campaign, establish a simple framework at session zero: are the characters poor, comfortable, or wealthy? What can they afford without rolling, and what requires effort? Keep it narrative. The Economy Facet module (planned) provides detailed rules for campaigns where trade, commerce, and resource management are central to the story.
