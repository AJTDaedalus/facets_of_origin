# Mirror Master's Manual: The Toolbox

This chapter is the drawer you reach into when the table is looking at you and you need an answer in the next five seconds. Who is this stranger and how do they feel about the party? What's the cost of that 7–9? What happened while they were searching the crypt? What's in the dead smuggler's pockets? Is the back door locked?

Each of those questions has a short procedure and a table. The procedures come first, in prose, so you know when each one applies and what to do with the result. The tables follow each procedure. They're printed from the same data the app uses, so the book and the app always agree.

> **Reading the Entries — the toolbox tables**
>
> Every table carries a **name**, the **die** it uses, a one-line **use** saying when to reach for it, and its **entries**, which cover every result of the die exactly once.
>
> **d66** means two d6 read in order, the first as tens and the second as ones, for thirty-six results from 11 to 66. **2d6** tables are read as a total, so the middle results come up most. **1d6**, **1d12** and **1d20** are single dice.
>
> Every entry is one line and playable as written. The tables are setting-neutral: no place, people or name in them belongs to any particular world. A setting Facet may add its own tables beside these, and you may add, cut or rewrite entries for your own table. They're yours.

Three rules govern every table in this chapter.

**A result is a prompt, not a verdict.** You read it, decide what it means here, and say that. If a result fights the fiction, reroll it, hold it for later, or ignore it. The table's job is to break the silence, not to run your game.

**Private until you choose.** In the app, every toolbox roll shows to you first. You decide whether the table sees the dice or only what happens.

**Offer three, choose one.** When you're looking for a move or a cost, read three entries (the one you rolled and its neighbors will do) and pick the one that fits. The app offers three for the same reason. You keep the choice, which is where the craft is.

## The Reaction Roll

When the party meets someone whose attitude the fiction hasn't already settled, roll 2d6 and read the band. Most results land in the middle, *uncertain*: they'll talk, but they want something first. That keeps most meetings from being fights and puts the next move in the players' hands.

Roll before anyone speaks, and let the result color how the person first appears, not only what they say. A wary patrol has its hands on its weapons before the party says a word. On an uncertain result, roll or pick what they want from Reaction Wants, or use the WANTS line on their card.

A hostile result doesn't have to be a fight. It means the fight is what happens if nothing changes, and the party has one breath to change something. Some talents move the roll: *Silver Tongue*, improved, shifts it warmer; *Beloved* keeps it from coming up hostile without cause; *Beast Friend* does the same for animals.

## Morale

Every foe has a morale number from 2 to 12, default 7. At the four triggers (the first of them falls, half of them are down, their leader is down, one is left alone and hurt), roll 2d6 once for the group. If the roll is over their morale, they break and do what their card's BREAKS line says. MM1 has the full procedure and Table MM1–3 for choosing a number.

Retainers check morale too, at the same triggers and whenever they're asked to do something dangerous that wasn't part of the deal (see *Retainers*, MM3). There's no table here, because a broken foe's card already says what happens next. That's what the BREAKS line is for.

## The Pressure Die

Roll a single d6 at the end of each exploration turn, at each leg of a journey, and when the party takes a breather somewhere that isn't safe. Every face means something: encounter, sign, local hazard, cost, opportunity, quiet. None of them is blank. MM2 covers running it in play: showing the sign before the encounter, writing a site's own faces 1 and 3, holding the costs for the first few turns.

Use the generic table anywhere. The four variants fit their ground more closely: underground, the wild, a settlement, and a social occasion, where the danger is people. For a site you care about, write your own face 1 and face 3 and use a variant for the rest.

## Moves and Complications

Two moments in play ask you to invent something on the spot: a player rolls **6−**, and something has to go wrong; or a player rolls **7–9**, and their success needs a cost. These tables are for both.

**On a 6−**, start with **Trouble**, the index of every kind of move: cost, position, attention, gear, harm, revelation. Pick the row that fits or roll it, then say what happens in the fiction. Never say the category out loud. If you want something more specific, go to the complication table for the scene you're in.

**On a 7–9**, go straight to the complication table for the scene: fight, exploration or social. Read three entries, pick the one that gives the table the most to do, and say it together with the success, as one package. The character gets what they wanted. The cost comes with it. It's never instead of it.

A move can be gentle or sharp. A gentle one shows trouble coming: footsteps, a creak, a look. A sharp one lands: damage, a lost item, the door shut. On a 6− either is fair. Use gentle ones when the players still have room to react, and sharp ones when they've already been warned and pressed on anyway.

The **one test for every entry**: does the table now have something to do? If a result only leaves someone slightly worse off, take the next one.

## Magic Costs and Mishaps

When a working rolls **7–9**, it works. Roll twice on **Magic Complications** (reroll a match) and offer both; the caster picks which cost to pay. The app does this for you. Narrate the cost as part of the working's success.

When a working rolls **6−**, the Fatigue is spent anyway and something from **Magic Mishaps** happens. The mishaps are written to make the scene worse and more interesting, never to end it, and every one of them is a natural Graceful Fail: a player who leans into it and makes it richer has earned a Spark. MM2's *Adjudicating Magic* covers scope, domains and Fatigue.

## Wounds and Scars

When a character drops to 0 HP they take a **Wound**. Roll a d6 on the Wounds table, or let the fiction choose if something obvious did the damage. The Wound fills one of the character's slots, and any roll that strains it is Hard. The entry's last sentence says what strains it. A night's rest in safety clears one Wound (see Chapter III.2).

A **Scar** comes only from the death choice, when a player chooses to live on rather than make a final heroic stand. It's permanent, and it changes what the fiction allows rather than any number. Roll on the Scars table if the player wants the dice to decide; otherwise choose one together, or write a new one that fits what happened. A Scar is a part of the character's story from now on. Treat it with the weight the player gave it.

## Stuck? Three Options

Every MM hits the wall: the party has solved what you prepared, or wandered somewhere you never pictured, or is sitting in a tavern arguing and the argument has gone flat. When that happens, choose one of these three. The app's **Stuck?** button offers one of each, taken from your own prep wherever it can find some.

**A threat moves.** Advance a Threat Clock one segment and show what that looks like, or show the next thing the villain is doing. The world didn't stop while the party was busy.

**Someone arrives with a want.** An NPC from your cast, or a fresh one rolled from the NPC tables, walks in needing something from the party, now. Roll their reaction if you don't know how they feel.

**A secret surfaces.** Something true the party could learn comes to them: a letter, a slip of the tongue, a body, a dream. Take it from your list of secrets for the session. If you have no list, take it from whatever you know that they don't.

Don't pick the most dramatic. Pick the one that fits the room. And if you find you're pressing Stuck? three times a session, that's a sign the prep is too thin in one place. MM2's *Prep by the Clock* will tell you which.

## The Oracle

Sometimes the question isn't what a character can do but what's simply true. Is the back door locked? Is anyone home? Did the guard see which way they went? When you honestly don't know, and neither answer is obviously better, ask the oracle.

Decide how likely a yes is and set the odds the way you set difficulty: **likely** is Easy (+1), **even** is Standard (+0), **unlikely** is Hard (−1), **very unlikely** is Very Hard (−2). Roll 2d6 with that modifier and read it on the same three bands the players use:

- **10+** — yes.
- **7–9** — yes, but: it's true, with a catch.
- **6−** — no.

A natural 12 is *yes, and*: true, and something more that helps. A natural 2 is *no, and*: false, and something worse besides.

When the question isn't yes or no (what's the rival doing tonight? what's in the locked room?), roll once on **Oracle Verbs** and once on **Oracle Themes**, and read the pair as a prompt. *Guard* and *a debt*: the rival has hired men to guard something she owes. *Lose* and *a key*: the room's owner lost the key months ago and has been sleeping somewhere else. Say the first reading that comes to mind; it's usually the best one.

The oracle is also the whole engine for playing without an MM, or with an MM who wants to be surprised by their own world.

## An NPC in One Roll

Someone the party talks to needs to be a person, quickly. Roll a **name**, a **trait** and a **want**. Say the name and the trait in the first sentence you describe them, and play the want. Roll a **secret** too, but keep it; it's for later, if they come back. If you don't know how they feel about the party, roll their reaction.

That's four rolls and about fifteen seconds. If the table takes to this person, the app will keep the card; promote them to your cast and give them a connection to one of the characters (see *People*, MM3).

The names are deliberately plain and setting-neutral. A setting Facet will usually bring its own lists for its own peoples, and you should use those when you have them.

## Loot

**Pockets and drawers.** Whenever the party searches a body, a desk or a pack, roll one **trinket**. It does nothing. It's a story, and it's the reason players search pockets.

**A hoard** is what's at the end of a site, in the villain's strongbox, under the ogre's bridge. Roll it in three parts:

1. **Coin:** 2d6 × 10, times the site's level. A level 1 cellar holds a few dozen coin; a level 6 vault holds several hundred. Remember that a hundred coin fills a slot.
2. **One curio.** Every hoard has one.
3. **A relic, on a 6.** Roll a d6; on a 6, roll a relic as well.

Then give the hoard a **provenance**, meaning whose it was and who will miss it, and put one trinket in it for color.

**Curios** are one-use and gone once used. A character carries three at a time (see Chapter IV.2). **Relics** are permanent: a power and a quirk. Anyone can use one, since the power belongs to the object and not the bearer, and carrying one never makes anyone a caster. Place relics by hand more often than you roll them, and write the quirk so it's also a hook (see *Treasure and the Reward Loop*, MM3).

## Usage Dice

Supplies that run out (torches, rations, arrows, lamp oil, bandages) carry a **usage die** instead of a count. It starts at d8, and each time it's rolled and comes up 1 or 2, it steps down: d8 to d6, d6 to d4, d4 to gone. Roll it after a scene in which the thing was used, and whenever the Pressure die turns up a cost. A refill or a fresh purchase steps it back up (see Chapter IV.1).

There's no table because there's nothing to look up. It's here because it's a procedure you run, not the players. Call for the roll, and say what running low looks like before it runs out: *the last torch is guttering, and the passage keeps going.*

## Writing Your Own Tables

The best tables at your table will be the ones you write. A site's own Pressure die, a rumor table for a town, a list of names for a people in your world. Four rules keep them working.

**Use the right die.** A d6 for a short list you'll roll often, a d66 for a long list you'll roll now and then, 2d6 when you want the middle results common and the ends rare.

**Cover every result exactly once.** No gaps, no overlaps, no "roll again."

**Write each entry as something happening.** Not "a bandit" but "a bandit counting coins she clearly just stole." A noun is a prop. A want or a situation is a scene.

**One line each.** If an entry needs a paragraph, it's a prepared scene, and it belongs in your notes.

The app will take a table you write in the same shape as these and roll it like any other. Keep the ones that work, cut the ones that don't, and over a campaign you'll build a drawer that's entirely yours.
