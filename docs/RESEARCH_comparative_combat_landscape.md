# RESEARCH: Comparative Combat Landscape

**Purpose.** Input to the Brain-tier design audit question: *"Has the system lost fun by chasing simplicity? How do other systems keep combat from being a slog while still making it fun and decision-rich? What do fast-combat systems do to keep combat interesting rather than merely short?"*

**Scope and method.** Twenty-one systems, each summarised as (a) license, (b) combat length and share of play, (c) the mechanisms that make combat interesting per minute, (d) what the community says is un-fun or slog-prone, (e) the one transferable *idea*, stated as a general principle. Then a synthesis that clusters the field by *how* they keep short combat interesting, and a short list of what fast-combat games are criticised for. License statuses and 2025-2026 developments were checked by web search on 2026-09-08; where a status could not be confirmed it is flagged.

**Copyright discipline.** Every entry paraphrases *concepts*. No move text, stat blocks, tables, or rules wording are reproduced. Per `CLAUDE.md`, the project may borrow *ideas* from any source but may borrow *text* only from openly licensed sources (CC, OGL, ORC, SRD-style open licenses). Bespoke "third-party" or "creator" licenses (Daggerheart, Draw Steel, Shadowdark, Mothership, Mörk Borg, Fabula Ultima, Cypher, Lancer, Savage Worlds) are *compatibility* licenses that bind a licensee to that publisher's terms; they are not compatible with redistributing text inside a GPLv3 project without a legal check, even where the license permits verbatim reuse. Treat all of them as "ideas only" for this project unless a later decision says otherwise.

This document does not audit Facets of Origin. FoO's current shape is used only to aim the comparison.

## Part 1: System entries

### 1. Dungeons & Dragons 5e (2014 / 2024)

- **License.** SRD 5.1 (2014 rules) has been CC BY 4.0 since January 2023. SRD 5.2 (2024 rules) was released 22 April 2025 under CC BY 4.0, with 5.2.1 following on 1 May 2025; WotC states all future SRDs will be CC BY 4.0. Open for text.
- **Length and share.** The best figures available: an EN World poll (89 votes) on "what percentage of D&D is combat" is spread almost flat from 31% to 80%, with the 31-50% bands and the 51-80% bands each holding roughly half of respondents; one respondent estimates two thirds of a 6-8 hour session. An EN World actual-play timing log at levels 2-3 gives ~6.9 minutes per round and 27-32 minutes for a 5-6 round fight, with one 10-12 round fight running an hour. Designer-side commentary treats three rounds as the "standard" encounter length under the 2014 CR math and ~5 minutes per round as typical. Sly Flourish's 2017 survey (n=1,165) found only 29% of respondents think 5e combat is too slow; his 2024 survey (n=2,800) found 78% run combat on a gridded map.
- **What keeps it interesting.** Build expression: class features are mostly combat features, so fights are where a character's choices become visible. A three-slot action economy (action / bonus / reaction) gives most turns one real decision plus a reflex. Spell slots and hit dice are a cross-fight attrition budget, so each fight's cost matters in the arc of an adventuring day. Grid positioning supplies spatial decisions.
- **Un-fun consensus.** The whiffed turn (a miss is a null event); monsters that are sacks of hit points with one interesting action; waiting 20-30 minutes between turns at tables of 5-6; high-level fights becoming slogs; caster decision paralysis; the adventuring-day treadmill (fights exist to drain resources, not to mean anything).
- **Transferable principle.** *A fight should be a showcase for choices the player made outside the fight.* When the character sheet has nothing that fires in combat, combat is where the sheet goes silent. Whatever the resolution system, the character's distinctive capabilities need to be the thing the player reaches for under pressure.

### 2. Pathfinder 2e (Remaster)

- **License.** The Remaster line (Player Core, GM Core, November 2023 onward) is under the ORC license. Open for text, with ORC's share-alike obligations on derived mechanics.
- **Length and share.** Comparable to 5e or longer. Paizo and community threads report 2-3 minutes per turn when players are prepared, but also hour-long waits in large fights with casters, minions and a boss. Share of session is not measured separately from 5e; PF2e is played as a combat-forward game.
- **What keeps it interesting.** The three-action economy is the well-known engine: every action is a unit, and the multiple-attack penalty makes a second and third attack progressively worse, so the *marginal* action is pushed toward something other than attacking (reposition, demoralise, aid, raise a shield, identify the creature). Four degrees of success (a result 10 over or under the target upgrades or downgrades the tier) give every roll texture. Conditions are a rich shared vocabulary, so support actions are mechanically real.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Cognitive load and condition bookkeeping; tight math that makes bosses crit players often; fights at low level feeling alike; a sense that there is an "optimal action sequence" and deviating from it is a tax; long turns at big tables.
- **Transferable principle.** *Make the obvious repeated action get worse and the varied action get better.* If a player's default is "Strike again," a diminishing return on repetition is the cheapest way to make the second and third choice in a turn (or exchange) a real decision without adding a subsystem.

### 3. 13th Age (1e 2013; 2e December 2025)

- **License.** 1e's Archmage Engine SRD is OGL 1.0a (open for text). Pelgrane joined the ORC alliance, and an ORC-licensed 2e SRD was widely expected, but as of this check no 2e SRD under ORC could be confirmed; verify before borrowing any 2e text.
- **Length and share.** d20-class fights, grid-less (abstract "nearby / far away" positioning), usually 4-5 rounds. Anecdotally shorter than 5e because of the escalation die and because fights are meant to be fewer and bigger (the game recommends roughly four fights per level with full recovery between).
- **What keeps it interesting.** The escalation die: from round two, PCs (only) add a rising bonus to attacks, capped at six. The designers describe it as poker blinds: the pace toward resolution accelerates every round. Fights start dangerous and become decisive; some class abilities unlock only when the die is high, so the late fight has more options than the early fight. Secondary texture comes from "flexible attacks" whose rider effect keys off the natural number rolled (odd/even, 16+, natural 20), so a single roll produces a stunt without a second roll or a declaration.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Still hit-point attrition; monster numbers bloat at high level; early rounds can be whiffy and late rounds trivial ("mop-up"); the escalation die punishes retreating and rewards standing and swinging; some GMs find that the die makes every fight have the same shape.
- **Transferable principle.** *Give the fight a visible, shared curve so each later exchange is more decisive than the one before.* A short fight that is flat is forgettable; a short fight with a rising line has an arc. A second, smaller idea: rider effects keyed to the natural roll create variety at zero cost in extra rolling.

### 4. Daggerheart (Darrington Press, 2025)

- **License.** Darrington Press Community Gaming License (DPCGL), a bespoke license: free, commercial use permitted, attribution required, whitelisted VTTs, no royalties. It is *not* Creative Commons. The SRD went 1.0 on 9 September 2025 and 2.0 on 25 August 2026. Ideas only for this project.
- **Length and share.** No initiative, no rounds. Reviewers report faster-feeling combat because nobody acts until they know what they want; there is no round cap, so fights can also run long. The game is combat-forward (reviews call combat "the meat"), so share of play is probably comparable to 5e in intent.
- **What keeps it interesting.** Every roll produces a resource for someone: two d12s, one "Hope" and one "Fear"; whichever is higher decides who benefits. Players spend Hope on abilities and help; the GM spends Fear to act with adversaries, interrupt, or fire adversary "Fear features." The spotlight passes to the GM on a failure or a Fear result, so the players' own dice set the enemy's tempo. Hit points are compressed: damage is compared to three thresholds and marks one, two, or three boxes, which is very close to a Resolve-style scale. Stress is a second, softer track. Adversaries have roles and each role has a distinct "Fear" behaviour.
- **Un-fun consensus.** The GM Fear economy is the sore point: a GM who enters a fight holding a full pool can chain adversary actions and lock players out, and the book's guidance on pacing Fear is felt to be incomplete. Quiet players can be lost in a spotlight-passing system at bigger tables. Optional "action tracker" rules exist because free spotlight breaks down for some groups.
- **Transferable principle.** *Every roll should hand someone a resource, and the enemy's budget to act should be visible on the table.* When the opposition's tempo is a pool the players can see (and their own rolls feed it), the fight has a felt economy even without initiative.

### 5. Draw Steel (MCDM, 2025)

- **License.** Draw Steel Creator License, a bespoke free license: rules text, mechanics and proper names may be reused; art and logos may not; attribution line required; amendments are non-retroactive. Not Creative Commons. Ideas only for this project.
- **Length and share.** Long. Reviewers say fights run "as long as D&D's" (1-2 hours) but with the qualifier that players are engaged the whole time; detractors describe "a three-hour chess match." It is deliberately a combat-first game with a separate negotiation subsystem for social scenes.
- **What keeps it interesting.** No misses: the power roll (2d10 + bonus) always lands in one of three tiers and every tier does something, so there are no null turns. A per-class Heroic Resource accrues every turn (and from class-specific triggers), so a hero is stronger in turn four than in turn one; the late fight is the spectacular part. Victories persist between fights and pre-load the next fight's resource, which rewards pushing on rather than resting. Forced movement is the main tactical texture; monsters have roles and captains; the Director has a mirror resource ("Malice"). Initiative alternates by side.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Tracking burden (conditions, triggers, resources), high prep, a narrative-light feel for groups who want story; it does not scale down to low-combat play.
- **Transferable principle.** *Escalation can live inside the character, not just in a shared die: a resource that grows each exchange makes the last exchange the best one.* And: *a reward that carries into the next fight (Victories) pulls play forward instead of toward rest.*

### 6. Powered by the Apocalypse (Apocalypse World; Dungeon World)

- **License.** Apocalypse World is proprietary (lumpley games); the Bakers ask only for a credit line and grant the PbtA logo on request. Dungeon World's text is CC BY 3.0. Open for text via DW only.
- **Length and share.** No initiative; fights are a conversation with a handful of moves. Typical PbtA fights resolve in 10-20 minutes and are a minor share of a session. Dungeon World, being D&D-shaped with hit points and damage dice, runs longer and is the more slog-prone member of the family.
- **What keeps it interesting.** The GM's move economy: on a 6- the GM makes a hard move, on a 7-9 the player chooses a cost, and when players look to the GM the GM makes a soft move. Monsters are lists of *moves* (things they do to the fiction) more than numbers. AW's own fighting move offers a *pick-list* on success: choose some of "you inflict harm / you suffer little harm / you take a position / you impress or dismay" — so a successful roll is itself a decision.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Dungeon World's own successors (the DW2 designers' "Think Dangerously" post) name the problems: the melee move is damage-centric and "inflict a condition" alone does not carry the fiction of a fight; the ranged move's costs could produce no fictional change at all; two moves are too narrow, so fights collapse into "I hack and slash again." Fight quality is GM-load: it is exactly as good as the GM's monster moves.
- **Transferable principle.** *A successful attack should present a small menu of effects, and every roll must change the fiction.* When harm is only one option among "avoid harm / take position / dismay," the player chooses what the strike *means*, and no result is ever null.

### 7. Blades in the Dark / Forged in the Dark

- **License.** Blades SRD is CC BY 3.0. Open for text.
- **Length and share.** Combat is not a subsystem; a fight is two to four action rolls inside a score. Fights are a small share of a session (no measured figure found), and are deliberately not the set piece; the score is.
- **What keeps it interesting.** Position × Effect, negotiated before every roll: the player and GM agree how risky the action is (controlled / risky / desperate) and how much it will accomplish (limited / standard / great), and a player can trade one for the other. The same d6 pool therefore means something different each time. Post-roll, a resistance roll lets a player pay stress to reduce a consequence. The devil's bargain offers a die for a complication. Tough foes are clocks; group actions and set-up actions make support real. Harm is a short list of levelled injuries, not hit points. Flashbacks let planning happen retroactively.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Bosses can feel weightless ("just tick the clock"); fighting-specialist playbooks feel less distinct because everyone rolls the same way; stress spirals; GMs new to the system struggle to invent consequences fast enough; the abstraction fatigues some groups.
- **Transferable principle.** *Let the player choose risk-for-reward before each roll and buy down consequences after it.* Pre-roll stakes negotiation and post-roll resistance together are what make a two-roll fight feel like a fight.

### 8. Fate Core / Fate Accelerated

- **License.** Dual-licensed OGL and CC BY 3.0. Open for text.
- **Length and share.** Conflicts are exchange-based with stress tracks and consequences. Community reports treat a 40-minute fight as slow; evenly matched combatants trade one stress at a time and stall.
- **What keeps it interesting.** Create-advantage-then-invoke: non-attack actions bank free invokes on aspects, so a set-up turn is visible progress and the payoff strike is decisive. Concession: a player can end the fight on their own terms before being taken out, and is paid for it. Consequences are lasting aspects that shape later scenes. Fate points flow from compels, so trouble is a currency.
- **Un-fun consensus.** The "Fate whiff" (equal skill means ties and stalls); create-advantage spam; fights feeling numerical when nobody narrates; consequence bookkeeping; reliance on the GM to compel.
- **Transferable principle.** *Build-up then cash-in, and a formal way to lose on your own terms.* A non-attack action that visibly banks toward a decisive strike gives the short fight a two-beat rhythm; a concession rule means "out of the fight" is a choice as well as a result.

### 9. Savage Worlds (SWADE)

- **License.** Proprietary. Pinnacle offers a free fan license (no approval, free products only), a DriveThruRPG community program with revenue share, and a formal licensee program. Ideas only.
- **Length and share.** Marketed as "Fast, Furious, Fun," and climactic fights that once took a session can take half an hour, but reviewers also report over an hour for a starter fight; whiff-and-soak loops are the reason.
- **What keeps it interesting.** Two-tier foes: named "Wild Cards" have wounds and a bonus die; "Extras" go down at one wound. Shaken is a soft state that costs a turn rather than health. Exploding dice make any roll a possible spike. Bennies (three per session, more for good play) soak wounds and buy rerolls. Card initiative with Jokers as a swingy bonus. Tests and tricks inflict "distracted" and "vulnerable" so non-damage actions matter.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Whiffing followed by successful soak rolls means "nothing happened" for several rounds; Shaken ping-pong (older editions); bennies feel too strong; combat is not as fast as claimed once many Extras, modifiers and edges are in play.
- **Transferable principle.** *A hard categorical split between disposable and named opponents, plus a soft "lost your turn" state that is not health damage.* Making mooks fall to one hit keeps fights moving; making a soft state cost tempo instead of hit points gives partial hits a feel without a number.

### 10. Into the Odd / Cairn / Mausritter

- **License.** Into the Odd: self-licensed SRD ("Mark of the Odd"), free reuse of SRD text with attribution. Cairn: CC BY-SA 4.0. Mausritter: SRD released CC BY 4.0 in June 2025, plus a bespoke third-party compatibility license. All open for text (Cairn with share-alike).
- **Length and share.** No attack rolls: attackers roll damage, armour subtracts, "hit protection" absorbs, then Strength damage forces a save. Fights take minutes. The design stance is explicit: combat is a failure state or a last resort; if you fight fair you *will* be hit.
- **What keeps it interesting.** The interesting decision is whether to fight at all and on what terms; certainty of damage moves all the tension to before the fight and to the save afterward. Morale rules end fights when foes break, not when hit points reach zero. Critical damage produces lasting scars that are also character growth. Mausritter's conditions occupy inventory slots, so consequences crowd out gear.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Some players miss the suspense of the to-hit roll (the "miraculous hit" moment); combat is short but flat; player characters feel powerless; fighting-oriented players have nothing to express.
- **Transferable principle.** *Make entering a fight the decision, and let fights end on morale or fiction, not on a counter hitting zero.* Short fights are fine when the meaningful choice happened one step earlier; and durable scars that double as growth make losing a fight generative.

### 11. Ironsworn / Starforged

- **License.** Ironsworn SRD content is CC BY 4.0 (with a separate CC BY-NC-SA for fan works). Open for text.
- **Length and share.** Solo and co-op oriented. A fight is a short loop: enter the fray, then alternate offensive and defensive moves against a progress track sized by the foe's rank, then roll to end the fight against the track. Five to fifteen minutes.
- **What keeps it interesting.** A binary *initiative state* that flips with outcomes: with initiative you make the safer offensive move; without it you make the riskier one; strong and weak hits decide who has it next. Ending the fight is a *roll against progress*, not automatic at track-full, so victory is a gamble you choose when to take. Momentum is post-roll agency: burn it to replace a bad roll, at the cost of resetting it.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Against tough foes the loop degenerates into "I strike again"; fights need narrative variety supplied by the player-GM; momentum burn feels safe and repetitive; some solo players want more tactical depth.
- **Transferable principle.** *A control state that flips with each outcome, so the same roll is offensive or defensive depending on who has the upper hand; and an end-of-fight roll so victory is chosen, not guaranteed.*

### 12. Year Zero Engine (Forbidden Lands, Alien, Mutant: Year Zero)

- **License.** Year Zero Engine Free Tabletop License, version 1.1 dated 31 March 2026: worldwide, perpetual, irrevocable while compliant, allows copying and modifying the YZE SRD. Open for text (check v1.1 for changes from v1.0).
- **Length and share.** d6 pools, sixes succeed. Fights are modest in length; Alien fights are short and lethal; Forbidden Lands adds card initiative, slow/fast actions and armour rolls and is the fiddliest member.
- **What keeps it interesting.** The push: after a failed (or weak) roll, reroll everything except ones and sixes, and every one that shows costs the character something (attribute damage, stress, broken gear). Alien's stress dice both add to the pool and raise the chance of panic, so pressure grows *because* the character is trying harder. Attribute damage doubles as hit points, so losing a fight shrinks the dice you roll with.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Swingy pools (ten dice, no success); pushing becomes the default rather than a choice; attribute-damage spirals make losing fights lose harder; Forbidden Lands combat is fiddly; successes lack texture beyond count.
- **Transferable principle.** *Post-roll "one more try" with a cost that lands on the character.* A pre-roll bonus die and a post-roll push feel different at the table: the push is the clutch moment, and the cost makes it a decision rather than a reflex.

### 13. Cypher System

- **License.** Cypher System Open License (CSOL): free, commercial, allows duplicating the Cypher System Reference Document; a bespoke agreement with Monte Cook Games, not CC. Ideas only for this project.
- **Length and share.** Players roll; the GM never does. Fights are moderate; the target is three times the opponent's level, eased by skills, assets and Effort spent from stat pools.
- **What keeps it interesting.** Pools are both health and fuel: spending Might to ease an attack is spending the same thing that keeps you standing. Cyphers are one-use items that rotate every session, so each fight tends to have a novel option nobody had last time. GM intrusion offers XP to accept a complication. A creature is one number (its level) that sets every difficulty involving it.
- **Un-fun consensus.** The "level times three then subtract" arithmetic; fights feel single-mode (everyone attacks); pool spending feels like slow bleeding; intrusions can read as GM fiat; high-level foes become whiff-fests.
- **Transferable principle.** *Loot as a decision, not a stat: one-shot, use-it-or-lose-it consumables that inject a novel option into every fight.* A game with no loot system yet can get most of the "what do I have this time?" feeling from rotating consumables rather than permanent gear.

### 14. Genesys / Star Wars (FFG, now Edge Studio)

- **License.** Proprietary. Edge's "Genesys Foundry" community program on DriveThruRPG allows original settings and content under program rules; the core book and dice are required. Ideas only.
- **Length and share.** Moderate to slow: building and reading symbol pools takes time, and reviewers report several sessions before a table reads the dice fluently. Combat share is similar to other trad games.
- **What keeps it interesting.** Two independent axes on every roll: did it succeed (success vs failure symbols) and what else happened (advantage vs threat, with rare triumph/despair). Advantage and threat are spent from a menu (recover strain, add a boost, inflict a critical, activate a weapon quality), so every roll narrates. Strain is a recoverable soft track; minion groups pool.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Interpretation load and lookup tables; "advantage fatigue" where tired players default to the same cheap spend; custom dice; a sense that everything crits or that outcomes are too swingy.
- **Transferable principle.** *Separate "did it work" from "what else happened," and give the second axis a short default menu.* A three-tier result already carries both, but the Genesys lesson is that the "what else" needs two or three ready-made options per context or the table stops inventing.

### 15. Mörk Borg

- **License.** Bespoke third-party license: free, attribution statement required, compatibility logo optional. Ideas only.
- **Length and share.** d20 against a flat difficulty; player-facing (monsters do not roll; players roll to attack and to defend). Fights are short and lethal. Play is short-form; combat is a large share of a short life.
- **What keeps it interesting.** Every roll carries a crit and a fumble (natural 20 and natural 1) with concrete, memorable effects. Omens are a small reroll pool. The world has a doom clock (Miseries) that ends the campaign, not the fight. Player-facing defence means the danger is felt on the player's own dice.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Lethality makes characters disposable; little build expression; fights are flat between crits; the game is a bit of a punchline for long campaigns.
- **Transferable principle.** *Crits and fumbles earn their place when their effects are concrete and gross, not just "double damage."* A natural result that produces a specific, quotable thing is a peak moment; one that produces a number is not.

### 16. Shadowdark

- **License.** Shadowdark RPG Third-Party License v1.1: free, commercial, no verbatim text except monsters, spells and items; attribution statement required. Ideas only.
- **Length and share.** Fast d20, roll initiative once and keep it for exploration too, theatre of the mind, ascending AC. Reviewers put a fight at roughly ten minutes. Real-time torches (one real hour) are the pacing device for the whole session.
- **What keeps it interesting.** Time itself is the scarce resource: table time burns torches, so dawdling anywhere, including in combat, has a cost. Spellcasting rolls can lose the spell for the day. Luck tokens are a light post-roll reroll. Initiative order as a permanent structure for exploration makes "whose turn" never a question.
- **Un-fun consensus.** The torch clock is called a gimmick: a one-minute fight in the fiction costs ten real minutes of torch. Tactically thin ("5e with fewer choices"); low player agency in fights beyond attacking.
- **Transferable principle.** *A clock outside the fight that keeps ticking during it.* Fights stay short when something the players care about is draining while they fight, and the clock is visible to them.

### 17. Dungeon Crawl Classics

- **License.** OGL 1.0a plus Goodman Games' free indie-publisher license (requires their approval); Goodman joined the ORC alliance in January 2023. Open for OGL-designated text.
- **Length and share.** Old-school fast at the roll level, slowed by table lookups (crit, fumble and spell-result tables).
- **What keeps it interesting.** Mighty Deeds: the warrior declares a stunt *before* rolling, and a separate visible "deed die" rolled alongside the attack both adds to hit and, if high enough, makes the stunt happen. One roll, two outcomes, freeform stunt, no extra subsystem. Crit and fumble tables by class make every natural 20 or 1 a story. Spellburn lets casters spend attributes for power. Luck is a spendable stat.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Chart lookups; swinginess; character death; bookkeeping for mercurial magic; "the fun is in the tables and the tables are slow."
- **Transferable principle.** *Let the attack roll carry a declared stunt whose success rides on the same roll.* Declaring intent first, then letting the die decide both the hit and the flourish, is the cheapest known way to make every strike a different sentence.

### 18. Fabula Ultima

- **License.** Fabula Ultima Third-Party Tabletop License 1.0 (June 2024): bespoke, perpetual, royalty-free, commercial, allows "Compatible with" and stand-alone "Powered by" works. Ideas only.
- **Length and share.** JRPG-shaped: HP/MP, status effects, side-based initiative. Boss fights are long by design (45-90 minutes); the book itself says conflicts should be reserved for dramatic moments and that clocks and checks can handle the rest.
- **What keeps it interesting.** Side-based initiative where the party chooses its own order each round, so sequencing is a team decision every round. A villain meta-currency mirrors the players' Fabula points. Bonds give bonuses. Clocks let the party defeat an unbeatable foe indirectly mid-fight. Non-lethal defaults and surrender/flight are normal outcomes. Attack rolls use two attribute dice and the *high roll* determines effect, so one roll gives hit and damage.
- **Un-fun consensus.** HP-sponge bosses; damage arithmetic and many status effects; prep-heavy; the JRPG texture is the content and does not suit every group.
- **Transferable principle.** *Side-based initiative with player-chosen order is a middle path between simultaneous action and strict turn order.* It preserves "no waiting for the GM" while giving the party a sequencing decision each round.

### 19. Cortex Prime

- **License.** Proprietary (Dire Wolf Digital since 2022). In May 2026 a new arrangement with Cam Banks put the handbook back on DriveThruRPG and allows him to sublicense third-party creators. Ideas only.
- **Length and share.** Dice pools from traits; total the two best, keep another as effect; ones are "hitches." Moderate pace, slowed by pool assembly.
- **What keeps it interesting.** The Doom Pool (in that mod): the GM's budget grows from the players' own hitches, is spent to oppose or complicate, and when it holds two d12s the GM may end the scene. Players can create assets and inflict complications as dice on enemies. Stress dice replace hit points. The fight has a visible fuse.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Fiddly pool building; a large doom pool makes easy tasks hard; "a toolkit, not a game"; pacing.
- **Transferable principle.** *A visible GM budget that grows from the players' bad dice and can end the scene.* When the players can see the fuse burning and know their own rolls feed it, a short fight has tension without a body count.

### 20. Mothership

- **License.** Mothership Third-Party License: bespoke, free, branding guidelines, no mandatory approval; 300+ third-party books. Ideas only.
- **Length and share.** d100 roll-under; fights are rare, short and lethal by intent; the designer's framing is that a fight is "one goblin with a knife" and the whole crew nearly dying, not twenty goblins a day. Avoidance is the game.
- **What keeps it interesting.** Stress accumulates on failures and feeds panic checks, so the fight gets worse the longer it runs even when nobody is hit: fear is the clock, independent of wounds. Player-facing rolls keep the danger on the player's own dice. Wounds are few and specific.
- **Un-fun consensus.** The Alexandrian's analysis finds 1e's combat procedure internally inconsistent: the text supports three different models (monsters roll; player-facing with GM-declared consequences; damage-on-failure with multiple monster attacks) and the designer himself acknowledges the guidance is unclear. When rules are underdetermined, GMs fill the gap with habits from other games and difficulty cannot be calibrated. Tactical players get nothing to do.
- **Transferable principle.** *An escalation track that is not health (stress, fear, exhaustion), so time in the fight is itself dangerous.* And a warning: *"NPCs never roll" needs an explicit, single procedure for how enemy actions resolve*, or every table reinvents it differently.

### 21. Lancer

- **License.** Massif Press' bespoke third-party license allows reuse of mechanics but not text or art; the core book text carries a CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 notice (non-commercial, share-alike). Neither is compatible with a GPLv3 project that may be sold; ideas only.
- **Length and share.** Grid-tactical mech combat, 2-4 hours per mission fight; a light narrative layer sits beside it. Community hacks exist specifically to replace combat with free-form resolution because it runs long.
- **What keeps it interesting.** Objective-based scenarios ("sitreps"): hold a zone, extract, escape, destroy, with round limits, so the fight ends when the objective resolves, not when everyone is dead. Heat as push-your-luck: overcharging risks meltdown. Two damage tracks (structure and stress) with cascade tables. NPC roles (striker, defender, controller, artillery, support) built from templates, so enemy composition is the encounter design.
- **Un-fun consensus.** Length; the gap between narrative and combat modes; prep; "a video game at the table."
- **Transferable principle.** *Give fights an objective and a round cap so they end on a condition, not a body count.* A three-exchange fight with a stated objective ("get the gate shut," "hold until the bell") has a shape; the same fight as "reduce Resolve to zero" is a race.

## Part 2: Synthesis

### 2.1 Seven ways the field keeps short combat interesting

**Cluster 1: Escalation mechanics that force fights to end.**
*Games:* 13th Age (escalation die), Draw Steel (Heroic Resources growing per turn; Victories across fights), Cortex Prime (doom pool with a scene-ending threshold), Mothership and Alien (stress/panic that worsens with time), Blades (clocks), Shadowdark (real-time torches), Lancer (round caps on objectives).
*Principle:* time inside the fight should be a rising curve, not a flat line. Whether the curve helps the players (escalation die, heroic resources) or threatens them (doom pool, stress, torches), each later exchange must be more decisive than the last, and the table must be able to see the curve.
*What a system without it loses:* the stall. Fate's equal-skill stress trades, 5e's high-level slog, Savage Worlds' whiff-and-soak rounds, and Ironsworn's "strike again" loop are all reported as the moment a fight stops being interesting; each is a fight with no rising line. Short fights avoid the *symptom* by ending quickly, but a two-exchange fight with a flat line is described by ITO/Cairn players as "flat" rather than "fast."

**Cluster 2: Rich decision-per-action structures.**
*Games:* PF2e (three-action economy with diminishing repeat attacks), Blades (position × effect chosen before each roll), Ironsworn (initiative state flipping the available move), AW (pick-list on success), Fate (create advantage then invoke), DCC (declare a deed, one roll decides both), Fabula Ultima (party-chosen order each round).
*Principle:* the same roll must be able to mean different things depending on a choice the player makes *before* it: how much risk, which effect, which stunt, which order. A decision structure is not more options on the sheet; it is a question the player answers each time.
*What a system without it loses:* variety of sentences. "I hit it again" is the universal complaint against DW, Ironsworn, Cypher and Shadowdark. When the only pre-roll decision is a stance chosen once per exchange, each Strike is the same sentence and the fight's texture must be supplied by the GM.

**Cluster 3: Post-roll agency.**
*Games:* Year Zero (push, with cost on the character), Blades (resistance roll paid in stress), Savage Worlds (bennies to soak or reroll), Fate (fate points to invoke after seeing the roll), Ironsworn (burn momentum), Shadowdark and Mörk Borg (luck tokens / omens), Cypher (spend from a pool, though pre-roll).
*Principle:* the clutch moment is after the dice land. A player who can see a bad result and *choose* to pay for a better one has agency at the exact instant the table is most tense. The cost must be visible and land on the character, or the choice degrades into a reflex.
*What a system without it loses:* the "I watched the dice decide" feeling. 5e, Cairn and Mörk Borg are all criticised for it, in different words: no miraculous save, nothing to do when the roll is bad. Pre-roll bonus dice (Sparks, Press, Borrowed Trouble in FoO's shape) add hope *before* the roll; they do not supply the post-roll rescue that YZE and Blades players describe as the peak of a fight.

**Cluster 4: Outcome-texture dice.**
*Games:* Genesys (success/failure plus advantage/threat), Daggerheart (Hope/Fear on every roll), PF2e (four degrees), Draw Steel (three tiers, never a miss), 13th Age (rider effects keyed to the natural roll), DCC (crit/fumble tables), Mörk Borg (concrete crits and fumbles), PbtA (7-9 with a chosen cost).
*Principle:* every roll narrates. A result should answer "did it work" and "what else happened," and the "what else" needs a short menu or the table stops inventing.
*What a system without it loses:* the null turn (5e misses) and the invisible partial. A three-tier roll already has texture, but if the middle tier resolves to "one less point off the enemy" it collapses to a number and the texture is lost in play even though it exists in the rules. Genesys' "advantage fatigue" is the warning that texture with no default menu decays into the same cheap spend.

**Cluster 5: Enemy-side texture.**
*Games:* Draw Steel and Lancer (monster roles, captains, templates), Daggerheart (adversary roles with distinct Fear behaviours), PbtA (monsters as move-lists), DW2 (enemies escalate when hurt), Savage Worlds (Wild Card vs Extra), 13th Age (nastier specials), Lancer and Draw Steel (phases and objectives), Into the Odd (morale ends fights).
*Principle:* the enemy must *do* things, not only *have* numbers. A role tells the table how this enemy behaves; a move-list tells the GM what to do when the players look up; a phase or morale rule tells everyone the fight has a shape.
*What a system without it loses:* interchangeable opponents. "Sacks of hit points" (5e) and "fighting the stat block" (DW) are the same complaint: the enemy is a durability number and the fight is a race to zero. Games where NPCs never roll (Cypher, Mörk Borg, Mothership) lean on this cluster even harder, because the enemy's only presence at the table is what the GM narrates; the Alexandrian's Mothership critique shows what happens when the procedure for that is underdetermined.

**Cluster 6: Danger and avoidance framing.**
*Games:* Into the Odd / Cairn / Mausritter (you will be hit; combat is a failure state), Mothership (one goblin with a knife), Mörk Borg, Shadowdark (torches make fights expensive), Blades (fights are complications inside a score), Year Zero (attribute damage shrinks your dice).
*Principle:* the interesting decision can be *whether* to fight. When entering combat is a choice with a known cost, a short fight is the punctuation of a longer decision, and retreat, morale and surrender are normal outcomes.
*What a system without it loses:* combat as the default verb. If fighting is always safe enough and always rewarded, players never consider talking, sneaking, or leaving, and every encounter becomes a fight the system must then make interesting on its own. Conversely, games that lean *only* on this cluster (Cairn) lose the players who came to be heroes: "powerless" is their word.

**Cluster 7: Reward loops that pull play toward or away from combat.**
*Games:* D&D (XP and loot from monsters; pulls toward), DCC and ITO (XP for treasure, not kills; pulls toward the dungeon but away from fights), Blades (XP for playbook expression and desperate rolls; coin from the score), Draw Steel (Victories reward pressing on), PbtA (XP on misses and end-of-session questions), Cypher (XP from intrusions and discovery), Fate (fate points from compels).
*Principle:* whatever the reward attaches to is what the table will do more of. Reward kills and you get murderhobos; reward fiction and the fight must justify itself; reward *pressing on* and the party stops resting; reward *trouble* and players volunteer for it.
*What a system without it loses:* a reason. If nothing carries out of a fight (no XP, no loot, no Victory, no scar that matters), the fight is either a mandatory tax (it happened because the adventure said so) or pointless (why not walk around it?). Communities of loot-less narrative games do not usually complain about *this* directly; they complain that "combat feels like a scene like any other," which is the same thing said kindly.

### 2.2 What fast-combat games are criticised for

1. **Samey fights / "I do it again."** Ironsworn (strike-clash loop against tough foes), Dungeon World (the DW2 designers' own diagnosis of the melee move), Cypher (single-mode), Shadowdark ("5e with fewer choices"). The fix in every successor is a menu of effects or a diminishing return on repetition, not more hit points.
2. **No tactical texture.** Cairn and Into the Odd (no to-hit roll, no manoeuvre), Blades (fighting playbooks feel undifferentiated), Mörk Borg. Players who want to *express a build* under pressure find nothing to reach for.
3. **Fights end before they get interesting.** Cairn players call fights "flat"; Blades players call bosses "just a clock"; Savage Worlds Extras drop before the fight has a shape. A fight can be too short to have an arc, which is different from being short.
4. **GM-load moves.** PbtA fight quality equals GM move quality; Cypher intrusions read as fiat; Genesys interpretation stalls at the GM; Mothership's underdetermined enemy procedure is filled by GM habit. When the system does not say what the enemy does, the GM's improvisation *is* the combat system, and it is uneven table to table.
5. **Lack of build expression.** Blades, ITO, Mörk Borg, Shadowdark. The complaint is not "too simple"; it is "my character's choices do not show up here."
6. **Abstraction fatigue.** Genesys' "just give a boost die"; Blades' clock-ticking; Fate's create-advantage spam. Texture that must be invented every time decays to a default under fatigue.
7. **No suspense without a swing.** Cairn's community explicitly misses the "miraculous hit." Certainty of damage is calm; calm is not always what a fight is for.
8. **Player-facing rolls without a written procedure.** The Alexandrian on Mothership: three incompatible models in one book. Any game where NPCs never roll owes the table one unambiguous sentence about how an enemy action resolves.

### 2.3 Where combat share and length have been measured or estimated

- **D&D (all editions), share of session.** EN World poll, 89 votes: 31-40% (18%), 41-50% (18%), 51-60% (17%), 61-70% (16%), 71-80% (16%), 81-90% (8%), 21-30% (8%), tails ~1% each. Median lands in the 51-60% band. A thread comment distinguishes "D&D as played" (30-40%) from "D&D as written" (70-100% depending on edition); a 4e group reports 95-99%. Another respondent reports two thirds of 6-8 hour sessions.
- **D&D 5e, length per fight.** EN World actual-play log (four players, levels 2-3, grid): 5-6 round fights at 27-32 minutes, ~6.9 minutes per round; one 10-12 round fight at about an hour. Substack designer commentary: ~5 minutes per round, most fights done by round 5-6, three rounds is the "standard" CR-built encounter. Sly Flourish 2017 (n=1,165): 29% say 5e combat is too slow, 71% say it is not. Sly Flourish 2024 (n=2,800): 78% gridded, 14% theatre of the mind, 8% abstract.
- **Pathfinder 2e.** Paizo forum and ttrpg.network threads: 2-3 minutes per turn when prepared; hour-long waits in large mixed fights. No survey.
- **Draw Steel.** Reviewers: "as long as D&D's"; "can take two hours but engaged the whole time"; detractors: "a three-hour chess match." No survey.
- **Daggerheart.** Reviewers report combat *feels* faster with no initiative; no timing data; combat is described as the main course of the game.
- **Fate.** RPGnet thread treats 40 minutes for one fight as a problem.
- **Savage Worlds.** Reviews: half an hour for a climactic fight when it works; over an hour for an entry-level fight when it does not.
- **Shadowdark.** The Alexandrian estimates about ten minutes of table time per fight against roughly one minute of torch time.
- **Lancer.** Community threads: 2-4 hours per mission fight; hacks exist to replace combat entirely.
- **Blades, PbtA, Ironsworn, Cairn, Mothership.** No measured share found. Design statements (Harper, McCoy, McDowall) place fights as a minor component of a score, expedition or session; anecdotal reports put a fight at 5-20 minutes.

### 2.4 Reading the clusters together

Three observations that fall out of the map, offered to the Brain tier as inputs rather than conclusions:

1. **"Short" and "interesting" are produced by different mechanisms.** Shortness comes from small durability numbers, mook rules, morale, and round caps. Interest comes from clusters 2, 3 and 4 (decision before the roll, agency after it, texture in the result) and from cluster 5 (an enemy that does things). A system can have all of the first set and none of the second; the community words for that state are "flat," "samey," "weightless."

2. **Every well-liked fast system has at least one of clusters 1-3, and the most praised have two.** Blades has 2 and 3. 13th Age has 1 and 4. Draw Steel has 1, 4 and 5. Daggerheart has 4 and 5 with a visible GM budget from cluster 1's family. Year Zero has 3. Systems criticised as flat (Cairn, Shadowdark, Mörk Borg) have 6 and little else, and they are explicit that flatness is the intent: the fight is not the point.

3. **Post-roll agency is the cheapest missing piece in most three-tier games.** A 2d6 three-tier roll already carries cluster 4. Pre-roll dice (Sparks, bargains) give hope, not rescue. Year Zero and Blades players describe the rescue, paid for on the spot, as the best single moment of a fight, and it costs one rule.
