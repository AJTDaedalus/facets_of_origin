# Mirror Master's Manual: Campaign Design

A session is a story. A campaign is what happens when a story starts remembering itself: the guard you bribed in the first week turns up in the eleventh with a promotion and a grudge.

Five things make that happen on purpose rather than by luck. A **frame** the players can pitch themselves into. **People** who want specific things. A **world that answers back** to what the party did last time. A **reward loop** of levels and treasure that lands the characters' growth where it matters. And an **ending**, the one most campaigns never get and the one worth designing for from the first session.

> **MM Note — where to see this chapter working**
>
> **Oraga Night** (`adventures/oraga_night/`) is a one-session module built to open a campaign. Its Overture states the frame, its cast chapter gives every antagonist a specific want, and its sidebars on what happens if history breaks show "the world answers back" written down instead of improvised. If you want to see the shape of a campaign's first night before you build your own, run it.

This chapter assumes you've run a session or two. If you haven't, start with a one-shot. A campaign isn't harder than a one-shot, only longer, and longer compounds both your good instincts and your bad ones.

## Campaign Structures

Every campaign has a shape, whether you choose one or discover three sessions in that you've been running a sandbox all along. Knowing the shapes helps you pick one, or recognize the one you've built.

**Episodic.** Each session is a complete story. The characters and the world persist; the plots don't. Best for irregular attendance, new players, and tables still finding out what they like. The risk is that nothing seems to matter beyond tonight, so thread the episodes together with recurring people, a home base, and consequences that echo forward even when the plot doesn't.

**Arc-based.** Arcs of three to five sessions, each with its own antagonist, stakes and ending, inside a larger story. Best for most groups: long-term payoff without perfect attendance. The risk is the gap between arcs, where the setup for the next one feels like homework. End each arc with a question rather than an answer (the villain is beaten, but who sent her?) and the question carries the table into the next one.

**Sandbox.** The world exists, and the players choose where to go. You fill it with interesting things and react to what gets poked. Best for proactive players and an MM who enjoys improvising. The risk isn't that nothing happens; it's that the players don't know where to start. Give them three obvious hooks in the first session, and let the world move whether they're watching or not (the faction turn in *Name Level*, MM3, is one way).

**Mystery.** The campaign turns on a central question the party slowly answers: why the old kingdom fell, what's behind the wall. Best for tables who theorize between sessions. You must actually have an answer, at least its shape, from the first session, because the clues have to be consistent looking back. If the players solve it early, that's not a failure. What they do with the truth is often better than the reveal would have been.

Most real campaigns are hybrids: an arc campaign with a mystery underneath, a sandbox with a clock ticking that turns it into an arc when the players finally notice. Use whatever shape serves the table this month.

## The Campaign Pitch

Before you build anything, write one paragraph. It tells the players what this campaign is about (the tone, the premise, what their characters will be doing) so they can build characters who belong in it. If you can't write that paragraph, you're not ready for the first session. If it runs to two, you're overbuilding.

The pitch is a contract. A player who builds a courtly diplomat for a pitch about crawling through tombs didn't fail. The pitch did.

> **Example — an episodic pitch: "The Thornwall Watch"**
>
> You're members of the Thornwall City Watch. Not the elite investigators, not the governor's guard, but the district officers who handle the things nobody else wants to. Every session is a new case: a missing merchant, a haunted tenement, a dockside brawl that turns out to be something worse. Equal parts procedural and comedy. Build characters with a reason to show up for work every day, even when the work is terrible.

> **Example — an arc pitch: "The Shattered Road"**
>
> The trade road between two cities has failed. Not collapsed: failed. Caravans turn back, travelers vanish, and the settlements along the way have gone quiet. You've been hired to find out why, reopen the road, and deal with whatever's responsible. Each settlement is its own story, and the cause will come clear the deeper you travel. Build characters with a reason to be on the road and a reason to care about the people along it.

## People

### Want, Secret, Connection

The NPCs your players remember won't be the ones with the best cards. They'll be the ones who felt like people: who wanted something, who surprised the table, who came back when nobody expected them to.

Give any NPC who'll appear more than once three things:

**One want.** Something specific they're pursuing right now. The archivist wants to retire before the new governor arrives. The blacksmith wants her daughter out of town before the harvest festival.

**One secret.** Something true they aren't sharing, which would change how the party sees them. The cheerful innkeeper owes money to someone dangerous. The priest hasn't felt their faith answer in three years, and it terrifies them.

**One connection to a character.** This is what makes the NPC matter to the table and not only to the world. It can be set at creation or found in play: "you know that pendant; it's from your village."

The NPC Wants and NPC Secrets tables in MM6 will give you a want and a secret in two rolls when you need them fast.

### Thirty Seconds to Memorable

Most NPCs don't need a history. They need to be instantly distinct. The trick is one concrete detail, not a personality summary: "He introduces himself mid-apple and doesn't stop eating it for the whole conversation." One detail is enough. The players fill in the rest, and they'll remember the apple for a year. The NPC Traits table has thirty-six of these.

### Recurring and Passing

Passing NPCs serve tonight: a name and a detail, no more. Some will get promoted without your help. The shopkeeper you improvised who made the table laugh will come back; the one you spent an hour designing won't. Accept it.

Recurring NPCs are characters in the campaign. After each session, ask what each of them thinks about what just happened. If you can't answer, they're scenery with a name. When the table latches onto someone, give them a want, a secret and a connection, and now they're part of the story.

## The World at the Right Scale

### Start With the Town

New MMs build from the top down: the creation myth, the gods, three continents' worth of politics. It's fun, and almost none of it reaches the table. Your players won't ask about trade between kingdoms they've never visited. They'll ask what the tavern's called, whether the smith can fix Mordai's sword, and why that old woman keeps staring at them.

Build outward from what the players can see. The first session needs a **place** with a name, two or three landmarks and one thing wrong with it; **three people**: one with information, one who wants something from the party, one watching; and **one problem** that makes the evening a story instead of a tour.

Think of the world as rings around the party. The **inner ring** (the town, the ruin) is fully prepped. The **middle ring** (the neighbors, the nearest city, the factions in the area) has names and a sentence each. The **outer ring** (distant lands, old history) is broad strokes. When a player asks about the outer ring, make something up and write it down; it's true now. As the party moves, the rings move with them, and you detail each place when they get there, not before.

### When Your World Has Its Own Peoples

The core rules ship one lineage, Human. If your world has others, they belong in a **setting Facet**, not in your head. A gifted lineage gives its members a **gift knack**, and may let them work Minor magic in one domain (see Chapter II.5). A lineage is a people, not a profession. If your draft reads like a job, you've written a background.

When you write the Facet up, lead with what it adds, counted, in one sentence ("this Facet adds four lineages, two classes and a table of rumors; it changes no rule") so a player knows the whole mechanical footprint of your setting before reading a word of its geography.

### Prep or Improvise

**Prep** when the players have said where they're going next, when you're introducing a major NPC, when there's a set-piece fight, or when a mystery's clues have to be consistent. **Improvise** when they go somewhere unexpected, ask about someone you never planned, or solve the problem in a way that makes your notes irrelevant.

The cardinal rule of improvising: **write it down.** The name you invent at the table is now the name. Your future self will be grateful for the three words in the margin.

## Levels and Pacing

### You Call the Level

Characters in this game have levels from 1 to 10, and you decide when they go up. There's no experience to count. At the end of each session, look at how far the campaign has come and at what the table did tonight, and decide whether it's time.

The default pacing, which matches how long real campaigns tend to run, is this:

**Table MM3–1: Default Level Pacing**

| Level | Reached after session |
|---|---|
| 2 | 1 |
| 3 | 3 |
| 4 | 5 |
| 5 | 7 |
| 6 | 10 |
| 7 | 13 |
| 8 | 16 |
| 9 | 19 |
| 10 | 22 |

The early levels come fast on purpose. Level 2 after the first session tells the players that growth is real. Level 3, after the third, is when each character takes their **signature**, the talent that says who they are, and it's the last moment a player may rebuild their character freely. After that, one level every two or three sessions.

### The Five Questions

At the end of every session the app asks the table five questions:

1. *Did we discover something new about the world?*
2. *Did we bring treasure home?*
3. *Did someone pursue their character's goal?*
4. *Did we change the world, for better or worse?*
5. *Did someone make a moment the whole table will remember?*

These are the reward loop. Nothing in them counts fights won or foes killed. They count exploring, treasure, goals, consequences and play worth remembering, which is what you want the players to chase.

Use them to steer the pacing, not to replace it. A session that answers yes to most of them is a session where the level lands on time, or a session early if the table is ready. A session that answers no to all five was probably a slow one, and it's fine to hold the level a week. Say the answers out loud with the table; it's a two-minute ritual, and it reminds everyone what the game rewards.

### What a Level Gives

Every level adds HP and one pick: a new talent from the character's Facet, or the improved form of a talent they've held for at least a level. Levels 4 and 8 add +1 to a stat. Casters name another signature working at levels 5 and 9. Every character adds +1 to damage at level 3, +2 at 6 and +3 at 9. The full rules are in Chapter II.4. What matters to you is that every level gives each character a bigger number and a new thing to do, so every level-up is worth a moment at the table.

**Talents from another Facet need a teacher found in play.** That's yours to place, and it's one of the best hooks the rules hand you. A teacher is an NPC with a want: the old sword-master who wants her name cleared, the hedge-scholar who wants a book returned. Put them where the player can find them, and let the lesson cost a favor.

### Levels as Story

Levels are also a rough gauge of how wide the party's world should be.

**Table MM3–2: Levels and the Size of the World**

| Levels | The party is | The trouble is |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Unknown | Local: a village, a street, one ruin |
| 3–4 | Known in town | A town's trouble, and a name or two who know theirs |
| 5–6 | Known in the region | Factions, roads, a city's politics |
| 7–8 | Noticed by the powerful | Wars, old powers waking, other people's kings |
| 9–10 | Powers themselves | Their own hold, school or following, and what threatens it |

### Faster, Slower

For a one-shot, start at level 1 and don't level at all, or start the pregens at 3 so everyone has their signature. For a short campaign of eight sessions or so, level after every session until level 5 and stop there. For a long one, stretch the gaps past session 10 to three or four sessions.

Whatever the pace, the cost of speeding up is that the ladder runs out; the cost of slowing down is that the players stop feeling it. Once you've picked a pace, say it at session zero, so nobody wonders.

## Treasure and the Reward Loop

Treasure is the cheapest reward in the game and the one that carries forward. It's also the second of the five questions, which is deliberate: a party that knows treasure brought home counts toward growth will scout, sneak, bargain and explore, and it will treat fights as obstacles rather than the point.

### What the Party Finds

**Coin** is one number. It buys gear, bribes, rooms, favors and a better class of trouble (see Chapter IV.1 for prices). A hundred coin takes up a slot, so a big haul is a real problem of carrying.

**Trinkets** are things with no use but their story: a spoon engraved *for good behavior*, an IOU for twelve chickens. Every pocket searched yields one. They cost nothing and players love them.

**Curios** are one-use wonders: a salve that mends 1d6 HP, a flask that makes one exchange's attacks Hard, a coin that answers one question truly. A character can carry three at a time (see Chapter IV.2). Put one in every hoard. Because they're used up, there's always a reason to find the next one, and because they're strange, they get used in ways you didn't plan.

**Relics** are rare and permanent: a power and a quirk. The lantern that lights the way back to where you last slept, and gutters out whenever someone in its light tells a lie. Anyone can use a relic. The power belongs to the object, not the bearer, and carrying one never makes anyone a caster. Place relics by hand more often than you roll them, one or two an arc, and write the quirk so it's also a hook.

Chapter IV.2 has the player's side of all this. The tables, and the procedure for rolling a hoard, are in MM6.

### Making It Matter

**Give treasure provenance.** Every hoard belonged to someone. A chest of coin stamped with a guild's seal is a story; forty coins is a number. (Who's missing it? Who'll recognize it being spent?)

**Give coin somewhere to go.** A party with a thousand coin and nothing to buy has been told treasure doesn't matter. Put things worth buying in front of them: a ship, a pardon, a teacher's fee, the deed to the mill. The best coin sinks are the characters' own goals.

**Hand out the strange, not the bigger.** Nothing in the treasure tables adds +1 to anything forever, and that's on purpose. An item that does something odd produces play. An item that only makes a number bigger produces nothing but a slightly different number.

## Sequencing the Danger

### Difficulty Should Breathe

A campaign where every fight is hard is exhausting; one where every fight is a skirmish is dull. Difficulty should rise and fall like breathing, with the peaks getting higher as an arc goes on. Using the fight sizes from MM1, a four-session arc might run like this:

**Table MM3–3: Danger Across a Four-Session Arc**

| Session | The fights | What they're for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A skirmish, or none | Establish the situation; let the party look around and plan |
| 2 | A real fight | First real resistance; the stakes come clear |
| 3 | Real or hard | Escalation; a setback forces them to adapt |
| 4 | Hard, with a way out | The climax; the arc ends |

That's a guideline. Some arcs put the hardest fight in session two and end on a negotiation, and some arcs have no fighting at all.

### What the Party Carries In

Every scene costs the party something: HP, Sparks, Fatigue, a Wound in a slot, a usage die a size smaller, an NPC's goodwill. By the climax, that total is the story of how they got there. A skirmish before a hard fight isn't about the skirmish. It's about the HP and Fatigue the party carries into the next room.

Across sessions it's subtler: the ally they insulted in session two who won't help in session four, the lead they didn't follow. Keep track of it in your sense of the fiction rather than a ledger, and let the climax show what they spent to get there.

### Not Every Session Needs a Fight

Combat is a tool, not an obligation. If every session has a fight, your players will expect one and stop being surprised by it. In a four-session arc, two or three sessions with fighting is plenty; at least one should be mostly investigation, talk or exploration. Ask your table which they want more of.

## Retainers

Sooner or later the party will want help: a torchbearer, a guide, a couple of sellswords, a cook who'll follow them into a tomb. These are **retainers**, NPCs who travel with the party for pay or loyalty.

**Writing one.** A retainer is a small monster card: a level (usually 1 or 2), a role (Standard for someone who'll fight, Mook for a porter who drops at the first hit), HP from Table MM1–1, a morale number, a name and a want. Morale starts at 7. Loyal, well-paid, well-treated retainers drift up; ignored or endangered ones drift down.

**Paying one.** Price them from the guide in Chapter IV.1: a porter or torchbearer is cheap by the day, and someone who'll draw a blade for the party expects a fair wage and a share of what's found. Unpaid retainers leave, and they talk.

**In a fight.** A retainer's action is **Help**: the retainer adds a d6 to a character's roll (keep the best two) and shares the cost if it goes wrong (see Chapter III.1). Foes can target retainers, who take damage and drop like anyone else. This keeps retainers useful without giving you a second set of attacks to roll, and without taking the spotlight off the characters.

**Morale.** Retainers check morale at the same triggers foes do, and also when they're asked to do something dangerous that wasn't part of the deal. On a break they refuse, flee or demand more, whichever suits them. A character with the *Captain* talent keeps retainers whose morale is 7 + that character's Soul (see Chapter II.4c).

Retainers are NPCs. You play them, the players direct them, and a good retainer has opinions. The torchbearer who won't go past the third door is a character, and she'll be remembered long after the tomb is.

## Name Level: The Optional Endgame

Levels 9 and 10 are **name level**. By now the characters are powers in their own right, and the question stops being "can we survive this?" and becomes "what will we build?" A hold on the river road, a school, a following of the faithful, a watch-house, a trading company. It's optional. Some tables would rather keep adventuring, and that's fine. For those that take it, the endgame runs on one small procedure.

### The Faction Turn

A **faction** is any group that acts on its own: a guild, a cult, a rival house, a band of raiders, and, at name level, the party's own hold. Each one needs four things:

- a **name**;
- an **impulse**, one verb for what it always does: *hoard, spread, protect, devour*;
- a **goal**, written as a six-segment clock;
- a **rival**, another faction in its way.

Between sessions, roll 2d6 for each faction and read the result.

**Table MM3–4: The Faction Turn**

| 2d6 | Result |
|---|---|
| 10+ | Progress: tick two segments of its goal |
| 7–9 | Progress at a price: tick one segment, and the faction pays for it somewhere the party can see |
| 6− | Setback: its rival ticks one segment instead, or the faction loses something it had |

When a goal clock fills, the faction gets what it wanted. Say what that changes in the world, and give it a new goal.

Every result turns into **one line of news**. *The river guild has bought the old bridge. The raiders burned a mill at Low Ford, and three of them hanged for it. The temple is hiring guards and not saying why.* Put that news where the party will hear it: in a tavern, on a notice, in a letter from someone who owes them. That's the whole point of the procedure. The world moves between sessions, and the party finds out the way people do.

**The party's own faction** takes its turn like everyone else's, but the players choose its goal, and what they do in play can tick its clock directly. A session spent recruiting, building or negotiating for the hold is worth a segment or two. Its rival is whoever the players have annoyed most recently, which at name level is rarely hard to pick.

Keep the list short. Three or four factions are plenty to keep a world moving. Ten is a spreadsheet.

## Ending a Campaign

### Knowing When It's Done

Campaigns either end or trail off, and only one of those is satisfying. The signs of a natural ending are clear enough. **The central question has an answer.** **The characters have arrived**, having become the people the campaign was about. **The table's energy has shifted**: players are talking about what they want to play next, and scheduling is getting harder. That last one isn't failure. Stories end.

When you see those signs, say so: "I think we're near the end of this story. Does everyone feel that?" If they don't, listen to why. They may see a thread you've missed.

### The Final Session

Build the last session on three beats. **The approach**: the party prepares, and everything they've gathered (allies, relics, knowledge) comes together. Let them plan, and let them feel how far they've come. **The climax**: the central conflict ends, in whatever form the campaign demands, and the outcome reflects their choices across the whole campaign, not just tonight. **The aftermath**: the dust settles, the world is different, and the players get time to see it.

Then offer each player an epilogue: a sentence or two on what their character does next. Some want one, some would rather leave it open. Ask. When a player takes theirs, give it the whole table's attention.

### Don't Drag It Out

The commonest way to end a campaign badly is not to end it. The story finishes around session fifteen, nobody says so, and it limps on for eight more sessions of fading energy and growing guilt. If the story is done, end it. A ten-session campaign that tells a whole story beats a forty-session one where the last fifteen were an obligation. Every time.

## In Play: The End of the Road

*The party has spent sixteen sessions on the Shattered Road, reopening the trade route between Thornwall and the coast. The last confrontation ended in a negotiation, not a fight: Zahna presented the evidence, Mordai stood behind him looking like a very large visual aid, and Zulnut pocketed the merchant lord's seal ring during the handshake. The road is open.*

MM: "Three weeks later. The first full caravan in two years rolls into Thornwall. There's a reception: bunting, speeches, a feast in the market square. The three of you are on the platform. Zahna, you've been asked to say a few words."

**Zahna:** "Define 'a few.'"

MM: "Under four hundred. The governor was very specific."

**Zahna:** "I've prepared remarks."

(The MM closes their eyes briefly.)

**Mordai:** "Are they good?"

**Zahna:** "They're accurate."

**Mordai:** "That isn't the same thing."

*Zahna delivers a precise, thorough and deeply boring account of the road's history, the failures that closed it, and a seventeen-point plan for its upkeep. The crowd is polite. The governor's smile never moves, which is its own kind of achievement.*

MM: "Mordai, you're behind him on the platform. What are you doing?"

**Mordai:** "Watching the crowd for threats."

MM: "There are no threats. It's a party."

**Mordai:** "There are always threats."

(There aren't. He looks anyway. The MM decides this is the most Mordai thing that has ever happened.)

MM: "Zulnut, you're notably absent from the platform."

**Zulnut:** "I'm at the feast table. I've been at the feast table since before the speeches."

*He's eating with both hands, leaning on a pillar that happens to give him a clear view of every exit. He spots a pickpocket working the edge of the crowd, a kid of about twelve with very good hands, and does nothing about it except nod approvingly.*

MM: "The sun is setting over Thornwall. The road you spent four months reopening runs south toward the coast, and for the first time in two years there are lights moving along it. What do you do next?"

**Zahna:** "There are records in the coastal archive about an older collapse. I want to know what caused it."

**Mordai:** "Someone has to walk that road and keep it open. I'll do it."

**Zulnut:** "I'm going to finish eating. Then I'm going to have a nap." *A beat.* "Then probably south. Mordai will need someone watching his back."

(The campaign is over. The story isn't; it never is. But this part of it is finished, and the characters and the world are both different for it. That's enough.)

## Quick Reference: Campaign Planning

```
BEFORE SESSION 1
  - One-paragraph pitch, shared before characters are built
  - A structure chosen (or a deliberate hybrid)
  - Starting place: a name, 2-3 landmarks, one thing wrong
  - Three people: want, secret, connection
  - Pacing agreed and said out loud

EVERY SESSION
  - The five questions at the end; call the level when it's time
  - Treasure with provenance, and somewhere for coin to go
  - After play: what does each recurring NPC think of what happened?

BETWEEN ARCS
  - End on a question, not an answer
  - Faction turn: 2d6 each, one line of news each
  - Place a teacher, a relic, or both

AT THE END
  - Say it out loud when the story is done
  - Approach, climax, aftermath
  - Epilogues for whoever wants one
```
