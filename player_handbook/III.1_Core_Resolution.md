# Rules: Core Resolution

Two dice, added together, plus whatever your character brings to the moment. That is the whole engine. Combat runs on it, magic runs on it, and so does talking a harbour official out of impounding your cargo.

What the dice decide is not *whether the story continues* — it always continues — but on whose terms. Ten or better and you get what you came for. Seven to nine and you get it, and it costs you. Six or less and the world takes the next move instead of you.

Everything in the rest of this book is that sentence, applied.

## The 2d6 System

When the outcome of an action is uncertain and the stakes matter, you roll **2d6 + the relevant modifier**. The total determines the outcome.

**Table III.1–1: Outcome Tiers**

| Roll Total | Outcome |
|---|---|
| **10+** | **Full success** — you achieve your goal cleanly |
| **7–9** | **Success with a cost** — you succeed, but with a complication or consequence |
| **6 or less** | **Things go wrong** — the story always moves forward, but not in your favor |

This is the only resolution mechanic in the game. Combat, magic, exploration, social encounters — everything resolves on this same three-tier system. Learn it once, use it everywhere.

## Modifiers

Your roll total is **2d6 + attribute modifier + skill modifier + difficulty modifier**. Each component:

### Attribute Modifier

The Minor Attribute most relevant to what you are attempting provides the modifier. See Chapter II.2 for the full attribute list.

**Table III.1–2: Minor Attribute Modifiers**

| Rating | Label | Modifier |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Weak | -1 |
| 2 | Average | +0 |
| 3 | Strong | +1 |

**Major Attributes** are used when no single Minor Attribute clearly fits, or for **saving throws** (see *Saving Throws*, III.1).

### Skill Modifier

If you have a relevant skill, its rank provides an additional modifier:

**Table III.1–3: Skill Rank Modifiers**

| Rank | Modifier |
|---|---|
| Novice | +0 |
| Practiced | +1 |
| Expert | +2 |
| Master | +3 |

Skills are optional on any roll. You always roll with an attribute; you add a skill when your training directly applies.

### Difficulty

Before calling for a roll, the MM declares a **difficulty** — a statement about the circumstances, not a judgment about your character.

**Table III.1–4: Difficulty**

| Difficulty | Modifier | When |
|---|---|---|
| Easy | +1 | Clear advantage, weak opposition, favorable conditions |
| Standard | +0 | The default — uncertain outcome, no special advantage |
| Hard | -1 | Skilled opposition, poor conditions, acting against the grain |
| Very Hard | -2 | Extraordinary opposition, severe circumstances, nearly impossible odds |

The MM declares difficulty **before** you roll.

A Technique can then move that declared difficulty one step further in your favor — it never replaces the MM's call, only shifts the label the MM already gave you. At most one such step ever applies to a single roll, no matter how many Techniques you hold: a step whose trigger is something the roll already carries (a weapon type, a hazard, a field of study) applies on its own and the roll result names it; a step whose trigger is a judgment call — a hunch, a genuine coincidence — you declare yourself when you roll. Either way the order is fixed: the MM's label first, the one Technique step second, then the four-rung ladder holds the result (Easy cannot go easier; Very Hard cannot go harder).

Those descriptions are the rule. The table below is the calibration — the same four tiers against tasks you can picture, and against the rank that clears them most of the time. Use it when a difficulty call is taking longer than the roll would.

**Table III.1–5: Difficulty Benchmarks**

| Difficulty | A task that sits here | Who succeeds most of the time |
|---|---|---|
| Easy | Climbing a rigged cargo net; recalling which guild owns a common maker's mark; getting a bored gate guard to wave you through | Anyone. A Novice clears this roughly three times in four |
| Standard | Picking a good household lock, unhurried; identifying a poison by its smell; talking a creditor into one more week | A Practiced character, about two times in three. A Novice is a coin flip |
| Hard | Free-climbing a wet curtain wall in the dark; reconstructing a burned page from its ash; turning a hostile crowd | An Expert, more often than not. Anyone below that wants a Spark |
| Very Hard | Crossing a collapsing bridge carrying someone; naming the author of an unsigned cipher from style alone; making an enemy commander stand down in front of their own troops | A Master with a Spark spent, and even then it is not a promise |

A benchmark is not a ceiling. If the fiction says a task is harder than the table suggests, the fiction is right — but move one step, and say so out loud.

## Sparks

A **Spark** is a narrative resource you earn by doing something remarkable — and spend for an edge when the stakes are highest. Each character begins each session with **3 Sparks**.

### Earning Sparks

Sparks are awarded for moments that make the table sit up. They can be awarded at any moment — mid-combat, mid-scene, immediately when the moment happens.

**MM award.** The MM hands one over for exceptional roleplay, for creative problem-solving, or for playing your character's established traits and weaknesses at real cost. No ceremony, no waiting for a scene to end.

**The peer call.** Any player may call out "Spark?" for *another* player's moment. The MM confirms it. The recognition comes from the table rather than from the chair, which is the point of having it.

**Act break nomination.** After a major scene transition, the MM calls for nominations, and each player may nominate one other player for something they did in the scene just past. The MM should almost always confirm — this is social reinforcement, not gatekeeping.

**The Graceful Fail.** This one is yours to claim. On any 6−, narrate how you make the failure worse or richer for the story, and the MM confirms. Not every failure earns a Spark; this is for the failures you make worth having.

### Spending Sparks

Before you roll, you may spend one or more Sparks. Each Spark **adds a d6** to your roll; you then **drop an equal number of lowest dice**. The base remains 2d6 — Sparks shift the odds by replacing your worst potential outcomes.

> **Example — spending Sparks**
>
> *One Spark: roll 3d6, drop the lowest. Two Sparks: roll 4d6, drop the two lowest.*

Sparks are always spent **before** you roll — there is no spending a Spark after you see the result. Sparks are most useful when everything is on the line. A Spark spent on a trivial roll is a Spark you won't have at the moment that matters.

## Saving Throws

When something happens *to* your character — not something they choose to attempt — the MM calls for a **saving throw**. You roll 2d6 + the relevant **Major Attribute modifier**.

Saving throws use Major Attributes because the forces acting on your character are broad:

**Body** covers what acts on the flesh: a toxin working through you, a collapse to survive, cold to endure, a shockwave to keep your footing against. **Mind** covers what acts on the attention: an illusion to see past, concentration to hold under pressure, a magical compulsion to recognize for what it is, composure to keep when reality stops behaving. **Soul** covers what acts on the self: domination to resist, an identity to hold against corruption, grief or supernatural terror to endure, a will to stand against.

The outcome tiers are the same as any other roll. On a **10+** you resist completely. On a **7–9** you resist at a cost — partial exposure, a momentary lapse, something lingering. On a **6−** the effect takes hold, and the MM describes the consequence.

> **MM Note — Saving throw or skill check?**
>
> A saving throw is reactive — something is acting on the character. A skill check is proactive — the character is acting on the world. If the character chose to engage with the threat, it is a skill check. If the threat came to them, it is a saving throw.

## Contested Rolls

When you act against someone actively working against you:

**Against an NPC:** Only you roll. The NPC's relevant attribute informs the difficulty the MM selects — a perceptive guard makes your Stealth roll Hard; an oblivious one makes it Easy. NPCs do not roll dice.

**Against another player character:** Both players roll. The higher total wins the contest. On a tie, both achieve partial success — neither prevails cleanly, and the situation deepens.

---

## Group Rolls

When the whole party attempts the same task together — sneaking past a guard post, navigating a treacherous path, searching a room under time pressure — use a **group roll**.

**How it works:** Each participating character rolls the relevant attribute and skill as normal. If **the majority succeed** (partial success or better counts as a success), the group succeeds. If the majority fail, the group fails — but the MM should note which characters succeeded individually, as their competence still matters in the fiction.

A standout result within the group adds narrative color: the character who rolled 10+ on the group Stealth check was the one who noticed the loose floorboard and silently warned everyone. The character who rolled 6- on the same check stumbled — but the group's majority success means someone caught their arm in time.

**The lead roller alternative:** For some tasks, the MM may designate one character as the lead — the most skilled navigator, the best lockpick, the strongest climber hauling others up. Other characters provide **Support** (see Combat, Chapter III.3) instead of rolling independently. This works best when one character is clearly more capable and the others are helping rather than performing the same task in parallel.

> **MM Note — Group roll or individual rolls?**
>
> If every character's individual outcome matters to the story (each person searching a different room), roll individually. If the party succeeds or fails as a unit (sneaking past the same guards), use a group roll. When in doubt, group roll — it is faster and produces a cleaner narrative beat.

## When Not to Roll

The MM should only call for a roll when three conditions are met:

1. **The outcome is uncertain.** If success is guaranteed or failure is impossible given the fiction, there is no roll. A professional lockpick does not roll to open a simple lock with proper tools and no pressure. A character with Strength 1 cannot lift a portcullis alone regardless of what they roll.

2. **The stakes matter.** Something must change depending on the outcome. If nothing interesting happens on a failure, there is no roll — just narrate the success and move on.

3. **Both success and failure move the story forward.** A 6- should never stop the story. If you cannot think of an interesting consequence for failure, the roll should not happen.

> **MM Note — The "Don't Roll, Just Do It" principle**
>
> Facets has fewer rolls per session than most TTRPGs, on purpose. Every roll should feel like it matters — a moment of genuine tension where the table leans in. If you are rolling for routine actions, mundane conversations, or situations where the outcome is already determined by the fiction, the rolls are stealing weight from the moments that earn it.
>
> The MM's default should lean toward *yes* — let the character succeed, let the story move, and save the dice for the moments that crackle.

## Outcome Guidance

### Full Success (10+)

You get what you wanted, cleanly. The MM should not add hidden costs, surprise complications, or "yes, but" qualifiers to a 10+. The player earned this.

### Partial Success (7–9)

You achieved something, but not cleanly. The MM must name the cost *before* the player decides how to proceed. Useful patterns:

- *Social:* You get the agreement, but the source knows you wanted it badly
- *Exploration:* You find what you sought, but something finds you in the process
- *Investigation:* You learn one true fact, but the conclusion you draw is yours to verify
- *Physical:* The task succeeds, but it cost something — time, equipment, noise, position

The complication should always move the story forward.

### Failure (6-)

Something *develops* — usually in a direction nobody planned. The story always moves forward. The MM's job on a 6- is not to punish the player but to complicate the situation.

- *You get what you wanted — but so does something else*
- *The attempt reveals something unexpected*
- *The action works partially, with a cost*
- *The moment is real but complicated*

A 6- is the moment for the **Graceful Fail** — leaning into the consequence or adding something to the fiction that makes the failure richer. Claim it yourself; the MM confirms.

## In Play: The Front Desk

The archive scene from Chapter II.2 starts here, half an hour earlier, at a desk with a sign on it.

> **Example — one roll, three tiers, and a ruling said out loud**
>
> **MM:** The Thornwall Municipal Archive closes to the public at four. It is ten past. The front desk has a brass bell, a stack of request slips, and an archivist who has already put her coat on.
>
> **Mordai:** I explain that this is important.
>
> **MM:** How?
>
> **Mordai:** Loudly.
>
> **MM:** That's Persuade — Charisma 2, no rank, so a flat +0. She's leaving, she's heard it before, and you are large and shouting in a library. I'm calling that Hard, minus one. Saying it now so nobody's surprised later.
>
> *Mordai rolls `2d6 + Charisma (2 → +0)` and takes Hard's −1: a 4 and a 3, minus one. **6.***
>
> **MM:** She takes her coat *off*. Not to help you — to write your description down on a slip. You are now a person the archive knows about.
>
> **Zahna:** Can I point out that I'm not with him?
>
> **MM:** You can try.
>
> **Zahna:** I'm not with him, and I only need the Millhaven parish returns, and I know the shelf.
>
> **MM:** Knowledge 3, Lore at Practiced. You are asking a professional a professional question, and you have just distinguished yourself from the shouting. Easy — plus one.
>
> *Zahna rolls `2d6 + Knowledge (3 → +1) + Lore (Practiced → +1)` and takes Easy's +1: a 5 and a 2, plus three. **10.***
>
> **MM:** She gets the box herself. It takes her four minutes, and for four minutes the desk is unattended.
>
> **Zulnut:** *(delighted)* I would like to be near the desk.
>
> **MM:** (I know. I know you would.) That's Chapter II.2's problem. Roll it there.

Three rolls, three tiers, one difficulty called before the dice hit the table and explained in the same breath. Notice what the 6 did: it did not stop the party getting into the archive. It cost Mordai something that will be waiting for him later.

## Standing Rulings

Two questions come up at nearly every table. Both are answered here so the MM does not have to invent a ruling mid-scene.

### Acting on Unnarrated Details

A player may only act on details the MM has described. If the MM has not put a thing in the scene, a character cannot use it.

A player may always **ask**. "Is there a window?" "Is anything in here burning?" "How high is the ceiling?" Asking costs nothing, takes no action, and is encouraged — it is how a scene gets sharper. What a player may not do is declare an action that assumes the answer.

- *Legal:* "Is there anything on the desk I could throw?" — the MM answers, and the player acts on the answer.
- *Not legal:* "I grab the letter opener off the desk and throw it" — when nothing on the desk has been described.

The MM's default answer to these questions should lean toward *yes*, exactly as it does for rolls. Most of the time the right response is "sure, there's a window" — and now there is one, for everyone, permanently. The rule is not there to deny the player the window. It is there to make sure the scene the MM is describing and the scene the players are picturing stay the same scene, and to keep new facts entering the world through the MM rather than through a declaration.

Once a detail has been narrated — by the MM, or in answer to a question — it is part of the scene and any character may act on it.

### Trying Again

A failed or partial roll may be attempted again **only if the fiction has changed since the first attempt**. Rolling the same action against an unchanged situation is not allowed; the result of the first roll stands.

The fiction has changed when at least one of the following is true:

1. **A new approach.** The character goes at the task a different way — a different attribute, a different skill, a different tool, a different angle.
2. **New information.** The character has learned something they did not know on the first attempt, and it bears on the task.
3. **Time has passed** — and the passage itself cost something or moved the situation. The patrol rotated, the storm broke, the pursuers closed the distance.

If none of those is true, the answer is no. A new approach usually changes the circumstances as well, so the MM declares difficulty again before the new roll — it will often not be the difficulty of the first attempt.

> **MM Note — "What's different this time?"**
>
> That question is the whole ruling. Ask it back to the player. If they have an answer, they have a roll. If they do not, the first result is still standing and the scene needs to move on from it.

This exists to keep failure meaningful. A 6- is a development, never a dead end (see *Failure (6-)*, above) — the story has already moved, and the situation the character now faces is not the situation they rolled against. Allowing the same action to be re-rolled until it lands would erase that development and turn every roll into a delay rather than a turn in the story.

- *The lock did not open on a 6-. The same picks against the same lock: no. Forcing the door with a pry bar, or going back for the key you now know exists, or waiting out the shift change so you can work unhurried — each of those is a new roll, at a difficulty the MM sets fresh.*
