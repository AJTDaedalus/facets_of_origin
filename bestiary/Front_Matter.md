# Facets of Origin — Bestiary

*Nineteen things worth meeting, and what happens when you do.*

---

## What This Book Is

Something is on the road ahead and the party has stopped walking. You have about four seconds before somebody asks what it looks like.

This book is the answer to that. Every creature in it arrives finished: what it looks like at first sight, what it wants, how it fights, when it stops fighting, and what a party can do besides fight it. Nothing here is a rule. Hit points, damage, armor, morale and the exchange all live in the Player Handbook and the Mirror Master's Manual, and these entries use those words without re-teaching them. What this book adds is the thing a line of numbers cannot hold, which is behaviour.

The Mirror Master's Manual teaches you to build enemies. This is the book for the nights you would rather not.

**Every entry ends in a way out.** Not an escape hatch for the party. An alternative for the table. Every creature in these pages has at least one resolution that is not a fight, and where a creature genuinely has none, the entry says so and explains why. That is not softness. A monster with only one solution is a door with only one key, and doors like that are why players stop asking questions.

## Everything Here Is Original

Every creature in this book was invented for Facets of Origin. None of them is derived from, named after, adapted from, or mechanically modelled on any proprietary bestiary, and no stat block, name, or piece of lore has been carried over from a published game. The project's copyright policy is in `CLAUDE.md`; this book is written to it.

That has a design consequence worth naming. You will not find the familiar roster here, and the omission is deliberate. These creatures were built backwards from the way the game plays: from a roll whose commonest result is a success with a cost, from morale that breaks on dice the whole table can see, and from foes whose attacks are telegraphed before they land. A chalk hound cannot attack from a standstill because standing still is a decision a player can make. A latchman states its instruction aloud because listening is a thing players do, and rewarding it is cheaper than any trap.

Bring whatever else you like to your table. Reskin these freely. But these were grown in this soil.

## The Setting Question

**None of this is Shattered Origin canon.** These creatures are deliberately placeless. They live on roads, in archives, at thresholds, and in the half-finished work people leave behind: furniture that fits almost any world because it is about almost any world's leftovers.

Every family entry that could plausibly be reskinned therefore ends with an **Adaptation** line: what to change to seat it in a particular setting, and what to keep so the entry still works after you have changed it. (The chicken does not get one. The chicken is a chicken.) Take the line seriously. A glassback with its antlers swapped for antlers of something else is still a glassback; a glassback that will start a fight is a different creature wearing the name.

A reskin changes the words, never the numbers. If your version of the Bought needs to hit harder, it is a different card, and it should say so.

The one exception is the **Archive Guardian**, which was already canon before this book existed. It appears here as the Boss of the Latchmen, because that is what it is.

## How to Read a Card

Every creature is a card, and most of the card is words. The numbers come from two things printed at the top of it: a **level** from 1 to 10 and a **role**.

The level sets hit points, damage and attack bonus, straight off the monster level table in the Mirror Master's Manual. The role adjusts them. A **Mook** drops to any hit and fights as part of a mob. A **standard** foe uses the table as it stands. An **elite** has twice the hit points and attacks twice. A **Boss** has five times the hit points, attacks twice, hits a little harder, and changes when it is bloodied. Armor runs from 0 to 2 and comes off every hit it takes. The printed card does this arithmetic for you, so the only sum left at the table is how much HP it has left.

Now and then a card overrides a number, because the creature was always an exception. The chicken is the obvious one. An override is marked with a †, and the creature's file says why.

The rest of the card is how the thing behaves:

- **Wants.** What it is doing when you meet it. Most fights in this book start because a party got between a creature and this.
- **Special.** The one thing that makes it not a generic brute, written so you can run it with the ordinary rules.
- **When bloodied.** What changes when it drops to half its hit points. For a Boss this is its second act, and you should say it out loud.
- **Tells.** How the players can learn about the special before it bites. Give these freely. Information before impact is the whole courtesy of a fair monster.
- **Breaks.** What it does when its morale fails: flees, surrenders, bargains, or, for the fearless ones, the only thing that will actually stop it.
- **Twists.** Six one-line variants. Roll a d6 or pick one, and the same card becomes a different fight.
- **Nastier.** An optional harder version, for when the party has met this thing before and walked through it.

**Morale.** Every card has a morale number from 2 to 12. When the first of them falls, when half are down, when their leader drops, or when one is left alone and hurt, roll 2d6. Roll over the number and it breaks. A 12 never breaks, so on those cards the **Breaks** line tells you what ends it instead. Low numbers are not a weakness. They are the reason most fights in this book end with someone walking away.

**Attacks happen in the open.** At the start of each exchange you tell the table what each foe is about to do and to whom. When it acts, its attack is rolled where everyone can see it: 2d6 plus its attack bonus, a hard hit on 10+, a hit on 7–9, a miss on 6−. That is the moment the players' choices pay off. A foe telegraphed at the scout on the left gives the warrior on the right one exchange to decide whether to step in front of it.

## How to Read an Entry

> **Reading the Entries — creatures**
>
> Entries are grouped in **families**, and a family ships its whole ladder: the Mooks, the ones worth naming, and, where the fiction supports one, the Boss. They share one lore section, because they are one thing at three sizes.
>
> ***Italic block, first thing in the entry.*** Read-aloud. What the characters perceive on first contact and nothing else: no numbers, no name, no hidden lore. If your party approached from somewhere the text did not expect, change it; it is a sketch, not a script.
>
> **The lore.** What is true about this creature, stated as fact. Where it hedges (*some say*, *nobody agrees*), the hedge is deliberate and marks a genuine mystery you may resolve however you like.
>
> **The card.** Generated from the creature's `.fof` file, so the book and the tool cannot disagree.
>
> **What Characters Can Know.** A three-row box keyed to the outcome tiers. Call for whatever roll the fiction supports (usually Mind, with +1 if a knack such as the right trade or upbringing applies) and read the row the result lands on. Each row is written to be said aloud. **6−** is what anyone notices. **7–9** is what a knowledgeable character recalls. **10+** is the thing that changes the plan.
>
> **Encounters.** Two to four ready rosters, with an honest read of how dangerous each is for a party of three around level 1 to 3, including when the answer is "not very". At least one in each entry is not a fight.
>
> **Ecology and Adaptation.** Where it lives, what it does to a region that hosts it, and how to move it into your setting.

## Finding Something

`Finding_Aids.md` lists every creature that has a card, sorted by level and then by role. It is generated from the creature files, so it cannot go stale. The hushfall is not in it, because the hushfall deliberately has no card; it runs on a threat clock instead.

The short version, for the nights you need a number now:

- **Levels 1–2** are the small fry: every Mook in the book, and the Kindly One, who is not really a fight at all.
- **Levels 3–4** are the middle of the book: the standard foes, the latchman and the veteran soldier. One or two of them make a real fight for a new party.
- **Levels 3–5** hold the Bosses. Every one of them can be resolved without being defeated, and three of the four would rather.
