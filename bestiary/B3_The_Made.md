# B3. The Made

Things that were built to do a job, by people who are no longer available to say the job is over.

This chapter has one family in it, at three sizes, and it is the family this system is best at. A made thing does not want anything, cannot be frightened, and will not be reasoned with — but it *can* be read. Every one of them is running an instruction, the instruction is a sentence somebody actually said, and the sentence is almost always still satisfiable by a party willing to take it literally.

That is the pleasure of the chapter, and it is worth protecting. A latchman that arbitrarily refuses a correct reading is a locked door with extra steps. Give the players the instruction, let them work on it, and when they find the seam in it, let them through.

---

## Latchmen

*Something is standing in the doorway. Man-height, iron at the joints, with a face that is a plate with two holes punched in it. It has not moved since you came into the corridor and it is not moving now. When it speaks, the voice is like a drawer closing, and what it says is:* "This door is not to be opened before the eleventh bell."

*There has not been an eleventh bell in this city for sixty years.*

Latchmen were made to keep doors. Not to guard them — *keep* them, in the sense a clerk keeps a register: to know the rule about this door and apply it. They were commissioned in quantity by guilds, courts, temples, and municipal authorities across several centuries, they were extremely good at the job, and they have outlasted almost every institution that ordered one.

**They are still applying the rule.** A latchman's instruction was given once, verbally, by somebody with the authority to give it, and it has not been countermanded because the office that could countermand it does not exist any more. So the rule stands, unamended, in a building whose purpose changed twice and whose owners changed six times, and the latchman applies it exactly as stated to whoever turns up.

They are not stupid. This is the point most people miss and it gets them hurt. A latchman understands its instruction perfectly, including the parts that no longer make sense, and it will tell you the instruction if you ask. It simply has no authority to interpret, and no mechanism for concluding that a rule has stopped being useful. Ask a latchman whether the eleventh bell still rings and it will tell you, accurately, that it does not. Ask whether the door may therefore be opened and it will tell you, accurately, that it may not.

**The three sizes.** *Latchlings* are knee-high and were made in batches for interior doors and cupboards; there is no dignity in them and they know it. *Latchmen* proper kept the doors that mattered — vaults, chambers, the ways in. And then there are the ones commissioned for something that mattered more than a door.

**Latchling** · *Level 2 Mook* · One bolt-arm; the other arm is a hand

**HP** — (drops to any hit) · **Armor** 2 · **Attack** +0 · **Damage** 4 · **Attacks** 1 · **Morale** 12 (fearless)

*Attacks as a mob: +1 damage per extra Mook (max +4).*

**Wants:** Nobody through its doorway without the right token.

**Special:** BRASS — a latchling drops only to a hit of 10+. On a 7–9 the blow rings off it and it is still in the doorway.

**Tells:** Something small and brass sits in the exact middle of a doorway, and the dust around it has not been disturbed in years.

**Breaks:** Cannot be frightened. It steps aside, without a roll and without resentment, for correct authority (a seal, a phrase, a livery), and it stops for good when the door is no longer a door.

**Twists (d6):**

1. The token it wants is hanging on a hook in plain sight, on the wrong side of the door.
2. Three latchlings keep three doors, and each accepts a different livery.
3. The door it keeps was bricked up long ago. It keeps the bricks.
4. It is keeping the cupboard the party's confiscated gear was locked in.
5. It is jammed half-open: each exchange it can bar the way or hit, not both.
6. Someone in the building has been handing it buttons, and it now accepts buttons as authority.

**Nastier:** Its doorway leads to a latchman's door, and the latchman hears the brass ring.

**Latchman** · *Level 3 Elite* · Iron hands

**HP** 28 · **Armor** 2 · **Attack** +2 · **Damage** 6 · **Attacks** 2 · **Morale** 12 (fearless)

**Wants:** The standing order carried out, to the letter.

**Special:** THE STANDING ORDER — the first time it is engaged it states its instruction aloud, and the instruction is always the true one and always decades old. It acts on nothing outside the order: a party that satisfies the letter of it walks past while the latchman holds the door.

**When bloodied:** It stops following anyone and stands in the doorway, Defending (no attack; attacks on it are Hard), repeating the order to anyone who will listen.

**Tells:** It has not moved since you came in. When it speaks, the voice is like a drawer closing.

**Breaks:** Nothing to break. It stops when the order is satisfied, countermanded by the authority that gave it, or made impossible.

**Twists (d6):**

1. The order is "not before the eleventh bell", and no bell here has rung eleven in sixty years.
2. The order names a guild livery. One still exists, in a chest, in a family that will want something for it.
3. The order covers the door. Nothing in it mentions the wall.
4. Latchlings keep the lesser doors, and each will recite a different piece of the building's rules.
5. The order lets the owner's heir through, and one of the party might count.
6. The building has been divided up, and half the order's words now describe the shop next door.

**Nastier:** It holds two standing orders from two authorities, they contradict each other, and it enforces both.

> **What Characters Can Know — latchmen**
>
> **6−** — *"It's built, it's old, and it hasn't moved. Whatever it's doing, it's been doing it a long time."*
>
> **7–9** — *"A latchman. It's keeping a rule about that door, and if you ask it, it will tell you the rule — they always answer that question. It won't act on anything the rule doesn't cover."*
>
> **10+** — *"It's literal, and the rule is decades old. Nobody has updated it because nobody can. So read it like a lawyer: it says the door mustn't be opened, but does it say anything about the wall? About being carried through? About who counts as staff? These things have been beaten by a clever apprentice with a borrowed coat more often than by anyone with a hammer."*

**Encounters.**

- **The cupboard problem** *(two to four latchlings — easy, and barely that)*. Latchlings on the storage doors. They are an obstacle with a solution written on them, and they are the correct first encounter of the family because they teach the trick at low stakes.
- **The kept door** *(one latchman and three latchlings — hard for a new party)*. One latchman on the vault, latchlings on the side doors. The fight is winnable and completely unnecessary. Somebody in the party will ask the latchman what its instruction is, and the moment they do, the encounter changes shape.
- **Three doors, three rules** *(three latchmen and two latchlings — deadly on paper)*. Three latchmen, three instructions given by three different long-dead authorities, and the rules contradict each other. Satisfying all three at once is a genuine puzzle and an extremely good forty minutes. Fighting all three at once is a deadly fight against heavy armour and nothing to break, and the entry does not recommend it.
- **The countermand** *(no fight)*. Somewhere there is a person, or a document, or a successor office with a legitimate claim to the authority that gave the order. Finding them is an adventure. Bringing them to the door is the ending.

### The Archive Guardian

The Latchmen's Boss is not a bigger latchman. It is what the same commissioning process produces when the thing behind the door is worth more than the building.

<!-- statblock: archive_guardian -->

**Archive Guardian** · *Level 5 Boss* · Iron hands, and seven feet of weight behind them

**HP** 100 · **Armor** 2 · **Attack** +3 · **Damage** 10 · **Attacks** 2 · **Morale** 12 (fearless)

*Changes phase when Bloodied (half HP).*

**Wants:** The archive undisturbed, and nothing else.

**Special:** THE JOINTS — it was built by people, so it has joints. Each time a character lands a 10+ and takes a stunt aimed at one, its attack bonus drops by 1 for the rest of the fight, to a minimum of 0. The party discovers this in play; the MM does not announce it.

**When bloodied:** Reduced Mode. Whatever was weighing consequences shuts down, and what is left is more dangerous. Every hit it lands is a hard hit, and it fixes on whoever last hurt it and does not look away until one of them is finished. It is running on something else now. What that is, the party does not know.

**Tells:** Seven feet of iron-jointed patience, with eyes that make light instead of catching it. It walks the same line across the room, at the same pace, and it does not hurry.

**Breaks:** Never breaks; it was not built with any. It stops when it is destroyed or when the thing it guards is no longer in the room, and it lets anyone leave empty-handed by the door they came in through.

**Twists (d6):**

1. One arm no longer answers it: one attack an exchange, not two, until it is bloodied.
2. What it guarded was removed years ago. It is guarding an empty room and does not know.
3. Its patrol is fixed to the minute, and a party that watches first can walk through the gap.
4. A door on the far side can be sealed, shutting it in with the thing it guards.
5. Somebody came before the party and did not leave, and their notes are on the floor.
6. The floor is failing under fifteen years of its weight, and the right blow in the right place sends it through.

**Nastier:** Reduced Mode starts at the first hit that lands on a joint, not at half HP.

The Guardian was commissioned by a city government through a guild that has since dissolved, to hold one room. It has held it for fifteen years without instructions, without maintenance, and without any indication that it experiences the passage of time as a problem. It is not malevolent. It is doing exactly what it was told.

That is, in certain lights, worse.

**What makes it a Boss and not a large latchman** is that its instruction has no seam in it. Latchmen keep doors, and a door has a hundred edges to argue about. The Guardian was told to prevent unauthorized access to the contents of a room, and there is no clever reading of that sentence, no borrowed coat, and no successor office — the guild that could have countermanded it dissolved along with every record of who held authority over it.

So this one is a fight, or it is a problem solved sideways: structural weaknesses, the fixed patrol logic, a sealed door, the joints. A straight fight is survivable and very expensive. At level 5 it stands three or more levels above a new party, which makes every attack they make on it Hard, and there is a great deal of iron to get through. Parties that win it head-on win late, hurt, and out of Sparks.

> **What Characters Can Know — the Archive Guardian**
>
> **6−** — *"Seven feet of iron-jointed something, and the eyes are making light, not catching it."*
>
> **7–9** — *"Guild work — proper commissioned construction, and old. It'll be running its original instruction, and whatever that instruction was, it's about this room."*
>
> **10+** — *"There's no argument to be had with this one; the office that could have called it off doesn't exist. But it was built by people, which means it has joints, and a clean blow that lands on one takes something off its attack for the rest of the fight. And when it's half done it changes: whatever was weighing consequences shuts down, it hits harder, and it fixes on whoever hurt it last and doesn't look away."*

**Encounter.** *(the Guardian alone — very expensive, and intended to be solved sideways.)* It is one creature against three, and a lone Boss rarely finishes a whole party; what it does is take a long time and cost a great deal. Its two attacks an exchange mean somebody is always the target, and once it is bloodied that somebody does not change. It is not meant to be met head-on. Everything the party needs — the patrol logic, the joints, the room itself — is available before the first exchange to anyone who looks.

---

**Ecology of the Made.** A city that used latchmen extensively three centuries ago is a city with a slow, invisible problem: hundreds of doors that cannot be opened by anyone living, in buildings that have been sold, subdivided, and repurposed. Whole trades exist around this. There are people whose entire professional competence is knowing which long-dissolved authority commissioned which building's fittings and what form of words that authority accepted, and they charge accordingly. There are forgers of authority. There are three or four families who have kept a livery in a chest for two hundred years because it still works.

And there are rooms nobody has been inside since the instruction was given, in the middle of otherwise ordinary buildings, with something in them.

**Adaptation.** The commissioning body is the dial: a guild, a temple order, a colonial administration, a dead monarch's household, a corporation. The material is free — brass, stone, bound spirit, standing spell. Keep the instruction, keep the fact that they will state it aloud when asked, and keep the seam. A made thing that cannot be satisfied by a clever reading belongs at the Guardian's end of the ladder, and there should only ever be one of those in a campaign at a time.
