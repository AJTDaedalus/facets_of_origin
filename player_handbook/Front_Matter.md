# Facets of Origin — Player Handbook

*A digital-first tabletop roleplaying game, built to keep the rules out of the story's way.*

---

## How to Read This Book

**If you have never played a tabletop roleplaying game before**, don't start here. Start with the **Quick Start**. It gives you the one rule the game runs on, a character in ten minutes, and a short example of play. This book will make more sense once you have rolled a few dice.

**If you are a player**, you need less of this book than you might think:

- **Chapter I**: what the game is and how it thinks
- **Chapters II.1 through II.6**: making your character (stats, magic, Facets and classes, lineage, background)
- **Chapter III.1**: the core roll
- **Chapter III.2, Chapter III.3, Chapter IV.1 and Chapter IV.2**: adventuring, combat, equipment and treasure. Skim them now and look things up later.

**If you are the Mirror Master**, read the whole handbook, then the Mirror Master's Manual: MM1 (encounters and enemies), MM2 (session design), MM3 (campaign design), MM4 (running the table), MM5 (the quick reference) and MM6 (the toolbox of tables and procedures). The Bestiary is the third book, the one you consult rather than read.

**The Facet modules** listed at the end of the Table of Contents (Downtime, Crafting, Economy, Feats, Technology) are optional. No table needs them to play.

**None of the three books is a novel.** They are built to be consulted mid-session. The Glossary defines terms, the Index says where each term is ruled on, and the List of Tables names every table by designation. Tables are numbered per chapter, so Table III.3–2 is the second table in Chapter III.3, and the text always cites them that way.

## The Boxes

Six kinds of box appear in these books. Each has one job and one voice. A box never mixes a rule with a flavor beat, and a rule the game always uses is never boxed. If it is in a box, it is optional, worked, or meta.

**Through the Mirror**: the designers, speaking as *we*, about why a rule is shaped the way it is and what breaks if you house-rule it away.

**MM Note**: technique for whoever runs the game. It states a default, the dial you may turn, and the cost of turning it. Players may read them; nothing in one is a secret.

**Example**: a worked instance, usually with the recurring cast (Zahna, Mordai, Zulnut and their MM). Arithmetic is carried through to the total. In a scene of play the speaker tags are `MM:` and the character's name.

**Variant**: an optional rule. The game is complete without every one of them.

**Reading the Entries**: a legend for the catalog that follows it. It names the fields, their order, and what it means when one is missing. Every entry in that catalog has the same shape.

**What Characters Can Know**: three rows keyed to the three outcome tiers, written to be read aloud: what anyone notices on a 6−, what a knowledgeable character recalls on a 7–9, and what changes the plan on a 10+. These appear in the Bestiary.

## Credits and Acknowledgments

Facets of Origin is created and maintained by its contributors; the full list lives in the project repository.

Every sentence and every table in these books is original. The game does borrow *ideas*, and we would rather say so plainly. None of the works below is quoted, and none of their text appears here.

- **Dungeon World** showed that a three-band 2d6 roll could carry a whole fantasy game, and that a hit on a partial success is worth more than a miss.
- **Knave** gave us inventory slots that do double duty, and gear that tells you who a character is.
- **Cairn** gave us hit points as grit rather than meat, and the scar a close call leaves behind.
- **Into the Odd** showed how little a fight needs: damage that bites, and a save only when the danger is real.
- **Whitehack** is where the idea of knacks that never stack, and of casting paid for out of your own hide, comes from.
- **The Black Hack** gave us the usage die and the habit of letting players roll what matters.
- **13th Age** gave us the idea that a character's one unique thing belongs on the sheet, and that monster damage can come off a single table.
- **Necropraxis** (Brendan S.) and the overloaded encounter die are the root of our Pressure die.
- **The Alexandrian** (Justin Alexander) shaped the MM's side: prep that survives contact with players, and procedures instead of plots.
- **Sly Flourish** (Mike Shea) taught a generation that lazy prep is good prep, and the MM Manual listened.
- **Morrowind** gave us the shape of the class system: a broad specialization that owns the numbers, with the freedom to write your own class inside it.

The safety tools discussed in MM4 draw on the **TTRPG Safety Toolkit**, curated by **Kienna Shaw and Lauren Bryant-Monk** and freely available online.

Openly licensed material incorporated in future revisions will be credited here with its source and license, as the project's contribution policy requires.
